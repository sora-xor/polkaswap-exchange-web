SUMMARY

1.    Sierra Leone is rich in natural resources, including iron ore, gold, rutile, and bauxite, along
with fisheries, forests, and fertile agricultural land. However, due to gaps in macroeconomic
management and other regulatory issues, compounded by external shocks, during the review period,
the economy struggled to pick up significantly and improve the country's living standards. GDP grew
moderately from 2017 to 2019, peaking at a 5.3% growth rate in 2019. However, COVID-19 caused
a 2% contraction in 2020. Growth rebounded to 4.1% in 2021 but slowed in 2022–23 due to rising
energy and input costs associated with the war in Ukraine.

2.     Slow economic progress, evidenced by low per capita income (around USD 800), weak human
capital, and a narrow-based economy, has kept Sierra Leone classified as a least developed country.
Its role in global value chains remains minimal, mainly supplying raw commodities to China, the
European Union, and India (90% of exports in 2023). Intra-regional exports dropped from 5% in
2017 to under 1% in 2023, indicating limited regional integration. Consumer goods, including food,
electronics, and transport equipment, dominate imports.

3.    While some social indicators have improved in recent years, Sierra Leone's slow economic
transformation has kept the poverty situation acute. Between 1990 and 2022, life expectancy rose
by 16.4 years (to 60.4), schooling increased by 4.4 years (to 9), and the Human Development Index
grew by 45.9%, yet the country remains in the UNDP's "Low Human Development" category.

4.    Sierra Leone's macroeconomic performance featured persistently high inflation during the
review period, with double-digit Consumer Price Index rates. Inflation fell from 18.9% in 2017 to
11.2% in 2020, driven by the Central Bank's restrictive monetary policy. However, food and fuel
price surges following the Ukraine war, along with a currency depreciation after the leone's
redenomination, pushed inflation to 47.6% in 2023.

5.    Monetary policy effectiveness remains limited due to inefficiencies, including the Central
Bank's lack of full independence, as it continues financing government deficits (up to 5% of prior-
year tax revenue). Loose policies and inflation volatility erode investor confidence. To address these
issues, reforms are underway to strengthen Central Bank autonomy and restrict the Central Bank's
lending to the Government.

6.    Fiscal mismanagement, compounded by various shocks, has resulted in persistent public
finance deficits throughout the review period, with a notable peak of 11.1% of GDP in 2017. The
total debt-to-GDP ratio also increased during this time; however, in 2022, the IMF considered
Sierra Leone's debt levels sustainable, albeit with a high risk of debt distress.

7.     Sierra Leone's current account, traditionally in deficit, showed significant improvement during
the review period. After experiencing double-digit deficits (including 19.4% of GDP in 2019), the
deficit narrowed to 3.8% of GDP in 2022, reflecting a recovery in mineral exports. The trade deficit
remains the primary driver of the current account imbalance. Merchandise trade as a percentage of
GDP rose from 53% in 2017 to 77.6% in 2023, whereas services trade fluctuated between 12.6%
and 10.3% of GDP over the same period. The country's merchandise exports primarily consist of
mineral and agricultural products. The relative share of minerals further increased during the review
period, leading to the worsening of Sierra Leone's export concentration index between 2020 and
2023 from 0.226 to 0.386. Imports are dominated by food products, electrical machinery, electronic
equipment, and transport equipment. In terms of services, key sectors include transport,
telecommunications, computer and information services, and travel.

8.    To enhance the business climate, Sierra Leone joined the New York Convention on the
Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards. In 2022, it adopted a new Arbitration Act
based on the UNCITRAL Model Law, governing both domestic and international arbitration.

9.     Trade policy formulation and objectives saw renewed focus during the review period. The
National Trade Strategy, adopted in 2020, aligns with the country's broader development strategies,
aiming to drive economic growth and diversification through infrastructure development, support for
women entrepreneurs, and enhanced competitiveness. It also seeks to reduce the current account
deficit by promoting exports, with particular emphasis on leveraging preferential market access
schemes with the European Union, the United States, and China, as well as free trade under

ECOWAS. Additionally, the strategy underscores the need for full implementation and capitalization
of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), which Sierra Leone ratified in 2019.

10.    Sierra Leone showed its commitment to the WTO during the review period. It participates in
the Joint Initiative on Investment Facilitation for Development. In 2017, it formally accepted the
Protocol amending the TRIPS Agreement, along with depositing its instrument of acceptance for the
Trade Facilitation Agreement. In 2024, it ratified the Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies. However,
its record regarding the notification of trade measures has remained inconsistent since 2017, with
submissions limited to certain areas such as trade facilitation, export subsidies, and regional trade
agreements.

11.   The investment framework is largely liberal with minimal restrictions, particularly in small-
scale mining and land ownership. Several measures have been introduced to streamline investment
procedures. A key development was the establishment of the National Investment Board in 2022,
designed to function as a one-stop shop for investment facilitation, coordination, and promotion.

12.     While the overall structure of investment incentives has remained largely unchanged, reforms
were undertaken to enhance transparency and discipline in granting tax incentives. These incentives
range from general and sector-specific tax concessions to customs duty exemptions and non-tax
benefits, primarily aimed at fostering development projects and encouraging new enterprises.
Eligibility for these incentives is subject to local-content requirements outlined in the country's Local
Content Policy.

13.    Despite these reforms, Sierra Leone continues to face challenges in attracting substantial
investments that can drive economic growth and create quality jobs. One key constraint is the
reluctance of financial institutions to extend credit to businesses. The authorities indicate that the
weak insolvency and bankruptcy framework raises the risk for banks to resolve non-performing
loans, which has led to limited credit availability and high borrowing costs.

14.   A number of reforms have taken place in customs modernization. Sierra Leone has committed
to implementing 82.8% of the provisions under the Trade Facilitation Agreement, within a period
spanning from February 2018 to December 2027. It transitioned from ASYCUDA++ to ASYCUDA
World in January 2019 and introduced the Electronic Single Window in 2020, streamlining
procedures in import processes. A risk management system is in place to help Customs focus
resources on high-risk consignments. However, a large proportion of shipments still undergo physical
inspections, which undermines the intended efficiency of the risk management approach.

15.   Additionally, the current destination inspection scheme aims to assess the quality, quantity,
value, and customs classification for imported items. It has resulted in overlapping roles between
Customs administration and the private company managing the process, leading to high fees that
increase trading costs for businesses and discourage integration into regional and international value
chains.

16.    In 2022, Sierra Leone began implementing the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (CET) with
five bands (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 35%) simplifying its tariff structure compared to the previous
system, which had seven rates. As of September 2024, the average unweighted applied MFN tariff
rate stood at 11.8%, down from 12% in 2016. Agricultural products, as defined by the WTO, continue
to face higher average tariff rates (14.4%), though this marks a decline from 15.6% in the previous
review period. The average tariff for non-agricultural products fell slightly from 11.4% in 2016 to
11.3% in 2024.

17.   Various duties and charges apply to imports, including a goods and services tax, excise duties,
and other levies. However, the predictability of the tariff regime is undermined by frequent changes
introduced through annual finance acts. Furthermore, a significant gap exists between applied tariffs
and bound tariffs, with the latter averaging 47.5% and all tariff lines being bound.

18.   Sierra Leone grants duty concessions and exemptions under economic incentive schemes,
international agreements, special customs regimes, and bilateral agreements with certain mining
companies. However, exemptions are also granted on a discretionary basis, which undermines
transparency and further inflates fiscal expenditure. To address this issue, the Tax and Duty
Exemption Act was adopted in 2023 to rationalize the process of granting duty and tax exemptions.

19.    Import prohibitions and restrictions are in place, in accordance with international conventions
such as CITES, the Stockholm Convention, and the Rotterdam Convention, with additional
restrictions based on sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) measures.

20.   Sierra Leone has not established legislation or an institutional framework for anti-dumping,
countervailing, or safeguard measures and has never implemented any such actions.

21.   Exports are subject to various requirements and fees. Exporters are subject to an inspection
fee amounting to 1% of the f.o.b. value of goods for quality assessments, even though additional
inspections may be conducted upon arrival at the destination, adding to export costs and
undermining competitiveness. Export taxes and charges also apply to coffee, cocoa, palm kernel oil,
palm kernel cake, ginger, and piassava.

22.    Export controls mainly consist of permit requirements for environmental reasons, covering
trade in certain plants and charcoal. Exports of gold, diamonds, and selected agricultural products
also require a permit.

23.   Sierra Leone has 16 technical regulations in place, although none have been notified to the
WTO. The country lacks essential laboratory facilities, limiting the authorities' ability to inspect
imported goods against the adopted technical standards. In cases where quality concerns arise,
samples may be sent to a foreign country for inspection.

24.   Sierra Leone places significant emphasis on SPS regulations, primarily to enhance the quality
and competitiveness of its agricultural sector, as well as to protect the health of consumers. Despite
reforms during the review period, including the adoption of a Food and Feed Safety Act, some
agricultural trade regulations remain outdated. The authorities have plans to further modernize the
regulatory framework and strengthen relevant institutions. Harmonizing SPS regulations within
ECOWAS is also a priority to promote regional trade.

25.   Sierra Leone does not have a stand-alone competition law. Instead, the Ministry of Trade and
Industry addresses anti-competitive business practices and works with the ECOWAS Regional
Competition Authority to promote fair competition. A Consumer Protection Act was adopted in 2020,
and a National Consumer Protection Commission was established to safeguard consumer rights and
enforce fair market practices. While prices are primarily market-determined, the Government
regulates prices for rice, fuel, and flour. Telecommunications, electricity, and water prices require
regulatory approval.

26.   The Government maintains significant involvement in economic activities through the
presence of state-owned enterprises in almost all sectors. This has often led to inefficiencies. To
address the issue, a State Ownership and Governance Policy for SOEs was adopted to promote
competitive neutrality and reduce fiscal impact. No privatization has taken place during the review
period.

27.   New public procurement regulations were adopted in 2020. They aim to improve public
procurement processes, including promoting electronic communication and environmental
protection. However, their full implementation is still pending. Open bidding is the preferred
procurement method, though some entities still attempt to bypass competitive methods.
Sierra Leone is neither a member nor an observer of the WTO Agreement on Government
Procurement.

28.   The enforcement of intellectual property rights remains weak, with copyrights and trademarks
being the most infringed forms of intellectual property.

29.   Agriculture is the dominant economic sector, contributing two thirds to GDP and employing
three quarters of the labour force. The authorities have conducted a value chain analysis for key
products, including cocoa, oil palm, cassava, vegetables, rice, ginger, and poultry, with the aim of
boosting productivity and promoting value addition. The Government's agricultural strategies aim at
achieving self-sufficiency in staple foods, optimizing value chains for export-oriented crops, and
encouraging climate-smart agricultural practices.

30.    In the forestry sector, the Government has plans to promote carbon capture initiatives. The
fisheries sector faces challenges in controlling overfishing and illegal fishing, but certain provisions
of the 2018 Fisheries and Aquaculture Act address these issues, emphasizing international and
regional cooperation, as well as monitoring and surveillance.

31.   Despite Sierra Leone's abundant mineral resources, value addition remains limited due to
related challenges, including unreliable energy supply. However, in recent years, investments have
improved electricity access for businesses and households, which more than doubled from 16% in
2018 to 36% in 2023.

32.   Sierra Leone's financial sector is dominated by commercial banks, which hold 85% of total
assets. The state plays a significant role, owning two commercial banks that account for 30% of the
sector's assets. Financial inclusion is low, with only about 29% of adults having an account with a
formal financial institution or mobile money provider. A financial inclusion strategy is in place,
focusing on women, youth, rural populations, and MSMEs.

33.   In telecommunications, the Government aims to promote digital economy, particularly digital
financial services, and e-commerce, to reduce transaction costs and stimulate economic growth.
This is expected to be achieved through improved telecommunications infrastructure, skills
development, governance, and cybersecurity. Mobile, fixed-line, and Internet services penetration
saw growth during the review period.

34.    The transport sector faces significant challenges, despite efforts by the Government to address
infrastructural gaps through public-private partnerships (PPPs), particularly in road and airport
construction. Tourism is seen as a key area for diversification, with the adoption of the first-ever
National Strategic Tourism Plan in 2024. The Government is relying on PPPs to enhance the quality
of tourism.

35.    In summary, Sierra Leone's economy faced considerable challenges during the review period,
especially due to the COVID-19 pandemic and to geopolitical tensions and their economic
repercussions. Although mining has played a role in supporting the economy, it has not been
sufficient to foster sustained growth, largely due to limited value addition activities. For the country
to achieve meaningful economic transformation, the authorities must prioritize investments in
human capital to drive productivity, improve the macroeconomic management framework, lower
business costs through consistent trade facilitation reforms, and strategically promote the
development of the digital economy and e-commerce. In addition, addressing energy costs and
reliability, as well as utilizing PPPs for infrastructure development, is crucial for attracting foreign
investments to ensure long-term economic progress.

1 ECONOMIC ENVIRONMENT

1.1 Main features of the economy

1.1. Sierra Leone is rich in natural resources, including fisheries, water, forests, and minerals.1 Its
deep-water ports and adjacent transport corridors have the potential to reduce trade costs and
promote economic growth. However, despite these advantages, Sierra Leone is classified as a least
developed country (LDC) due to structural challenges that impede its economic progress. These
include infrastructure deficits such as inadequate affordable and reliable energy, weak human
capital, and gaps in economic governance, particularly in macroeconomic management and sectoral
regulation. The predominance of the informal sector also hinders economic performance, leaving the
economy largely undiversified. Moreover, Sierra Leone is vulnerable to climate-related risks, which
frequently disrupt economic activities. These risks include extreme weather events and declining
agricultural land fertility, further complicating the country's development trajectory.

1.2. With an estimated population of approximately 8.8 million in 2023, up from 7.3 million in 2017
(Table 1.1), Sierra Leone has a relatively young population.

1.3. According to the World Bank, the poverty rate in Sierra Leone, measured by the USD 1.90
poverty line (2011 PPP), was estimated at 43% of the population in 2018 based on data from the
Sierra Leone Integrated Household Survey (SLIHS). This marks a significant decrease from 54.7%
in 2011. However, the Gini coefficient rose from 0.33 in 2011 to 0.36 in 2018 (latest available data),
indicating widening inequality.2

1.4. Despite improvements over the past two decades, overall human development in Sierra Leone
remains persistently low. According to the UNDP, between 1990 and 2022, the country's life
expectancy at birth increased by 16.4 years (to 60.4 years), expected years of schooling rose by
4.4 years (to 9 years), and mean years of schooling improved by 1.6 years (to 3.5 years). Although
Sierra Leone's Human Development Index (HDI) value increased by 45.9% during this period, rising
from 0.314 to 0.458, the country remains in the "Low Human Development" category.3

1.5. The sectoral distribution of GDP in Sierra Leone continues to be dominated by agriculture,
accounting for approximately two thirds of total output. This share increased during the review
period, partly due to the declining contribution of the mining industry to the country's domestic
output. Crop production and fisheries are the main subsectors within agriculture (Table 1.1).
Services, particularly wholesale and retail trade, along with financial intermediation, remain the
second-largest sector of the economy.

1.2 Recent economic developments

1.6. Sierra Leone experienced steady economic growth from 2017 to 2019, driven by the
resumption of iron ore production and recovery in the agriculture, construction, tourism, transport,
and communications sectors. However, the COVID-19 pandemic led to a 2% contraction in 2020,
disrupting global demand for Sierra Leonean commodities and stalling foreign investment. Tourism
and hospitality were particularly affected. The Government's Quick Action Economic Response
Programme (QAERP), alongside sound macroeconomic policies, helped mitigate the impact.

1.7. Growth rebounded to 4.1% in 2021, as the mining sector recovered, particularly in iron ore
production. However, the war in Ukraine triggered increases in energy and agricultural input prices
in 2022-23, affecting further recovery.

1.8. No noticeable progress was reported with respect to the agreement establishing the West
African Monetary Zone (WAMZ), a monetary union to which Sierra Leone is party with 5 of the 16
ECOWAS members (the Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, and Nigeria). As indicated during the
previous Review, the launch of a single currency has been delayed many times.

1.9. Monetary policy in Sierra Leone falls under the responsibility of the Bank of Sierra Leone (BSL),
whose mission includes managing the national currency, the new leone (SLE), as well as formulating
and implementing monetary policy. The primary aim of monetary policy is to achieve and maintain
price stability.

1.10. During the review period, Sierra Leone remained a high-inflation country, with Consumer
Price Index (CPI) rates consistently in double digits each year (Table 1.1). The ongoing rise in CPI
was primarily driven by high prices of imported food and inputs, exchange rate depreciation, and
the subsequent pass-through to domestic prices. While inflation rates showed a decreasing trend
from 2017 to 2021, they surged in 2022 and 2023, largely due to spikes in food and fuel prices
following the Ukraine war and the depreciation of the leone that followed its redenomination.

1.11. On 1 July 2022, Siera Leone's national currency, the leone (SLL), was redenominated by
removing three zeros. The new leone (SLE) was introduced alongside the old one, with both being
accepted as legal tender during an initial six-month transition period. According to the IMF, the
redenomination announcement in August 2021 heightened uncertainty and speculation, exerting
downward pressure on the exchange rate and raising import costs.4 Additionally, the depreciation
has been partly attributed to exporters' non-compliance with repatriation requirements. The
authorities note that the Finance Act, 2023 introduced stricter control measures to ensure the
effective repatriation of export proceeds.

1.12. Notably, the inflationary uptick occurred despite the BSL's recent tightening of monetary
policy. The Central Bank raised its policy rate from 14.25% in December 2021 to 18.25% in
December 2022 and further to 22.25% in December 2023.

1.13. Efforts to achieve monetary policy objectives, particularly in controlling inflation, have been
hampered by inefficiencies in the monetary policy framework, largely due to obstacles in the
transmission channels. The IMF highlights that the significant disparity between domestic interest
rates  ranging from 2% for deposits to 23% for 1-year Treasury bills  suggests potential
inefficiencies in the system. One possible reason for weak monetary transmission is the Central
Bank's pursuit of competing goals, such as managing inflation while also addressing the
Government's budgetary needs. It is noteworthy that the BSL is permitted to address the Central
Government's fiscal needs by financing deficits up to 5% of the previous year's tax revenue. Fiscal

1.14. High and volatile inflation, coupled with a depreciating domestic currency, risks distorting

chains. Additionally, the authorities acknowledge that rising global inflationary pressures and their
spillover into domestic inflation present significant challenges to poverty alleviation efforts.6

1.15. The authorities recognize the importance of price stability for economic diversification. The
Bank of Sierra Leone Act, adopted in June 2019, seeks to improve the Central Bank's policy
effectiveness. This Act includes provisions that safeguard the personal autonomy of the Governor,
deputy governors, and directors, as well as enhanced restrictions on credit extended to the
Government.

1.16. The public finance situation was challenging throughout much of the review period. Tight fiscal
space, compounded by successive external shocks, weaknesses in budget controls, and the legacy
of past fiscal mismanagement, resulted in significant fiscal overruns, particularly in 2017, when the
overall deficit reached 11.1% of GDP. Recent expenditure pressures have arisen from measures to
manage a third wave of COVID-19, salary increases for teachers, rising costs for goods and services
due to inflation, and higher energy subsidies, including transfers to the Electricity Distribution and
Supply Authority. Additionally, Sierra Leone has accumulated a substantial stock of domestic
expenditure arrears, which may undermine business confidence in the Government.

1.17. A notable deficit reduction occurred in 2019, primarily driven by strong domestic revenue
performance following the implementation of the Treasury Single Account  a system that channels
all government-generated revenues into a single central account. Despite this deficit reduction, the
total debt-to-GDP ratio increased during the review period. However, the IMF indicated in 2022 that
while the country's overall and external public debts are sustainable, they remain at a high risk of
debt distress.7

1.18. Sierra Leone's current account has historically been in deficit, primarily due to a significant
trade deficit (Table 1.2). During the review period, the current account deficit as a percentage of
GDP eased considerably, declining from three consecutive years of double-digit deficits to 3.8% of
GDP in 2022 (Table 1.1). The substantial deficit between 2017 and 2019 was largely attributed to
declining export receipts following the closure of the country's only operational iron ore mine, which
was only partially mitigated by increased diamond exports. However, the deficit gradually narrowed
as iron ore production resumed and exports of diamonds, bauxite, and rutile picked up.

1.19. Sierra Leone's worsening current account balance highlights the country's reliance on a
narrow range of exports. While foreign direct investment (FDI) and external official loans have been
instrumental in financing the current account deficit and maintaining relatively adequate reserve
levels, implementing supply-side reforms is crucial for diversifying the economy, enhancing export
competitiveness, and broadening Sierra Leone's foreign exchange earnings.

1.3 Developments in trade and investment

1.3.1 Trends and patterns in merchandise and services trade

1.20. The value of merchandise exports saw a significant decline between 2017 and 2020, primarily
due to reduced activity in the mineral industries. However, a robust recovery began in 2021, and
exports have continued to grow since then, with the 2023 export value being nearly double that of
2017 (Table A1.1). The export basket consists primarily of mineral products (e.g. iron ores, titanium
ores, and diamonds) and agricultural products (e.g. coffee, tea, cocoa, and spices). Key shifts in
export composition included increasing exports of iron ores and concentrates, alongside a decline in
wood, paper, and furniture exports (Chart 1.1 and Table A1.1). Additionally, there was a decrease
in the relative importance of cocoa over the period. The increase in the relative share of minerals
exports led to a further concentration of total merchandise exports during the review period. This
reflected in the increase of UNCTAD's export product concentration index from 0.226 in 2020 to
0.386 in 2023.8

1.21. The country's merchandise imports experienced a progressive increase from 2017 to 2023.
Food products, electrical machinery and electronic equipment, and transport equipment were the
main components. Minerals and metals, in particular cement and ceramics imports, were also
significant (Table A1.2 and Chart 1.1).

1.3.2 Trends and patterns in FDI

1.25. FDI inflows were volatile during the review period, showing a clear downward trend. Inflows
nearly halved over the period, declining from USD 413.7 million in 2017 to USD 186.2 million in
2022, before rebounding to USD 263 million in 2023 (Table 1.1). The performance of FDI has been
closely tied to business sentiment in the mining sector, which remains the primary destination for
such investments. The recovery in FDI figures in 2023 reflects robust mining activities that resumed
in 2021, coupled with proactive government policies. For instance, the establishment of the National
Investment Board (NIB) in 2022 aimed to promote business investments in Sierra Leone.
Additionally, the Government's diversification efforts, focused on agriculture, fisheries, and
infrastructure, have started to attract investors to these sectors. The Government has also prioritized
public-private partnerships for major infrastructure projects, particularly in energy, roads, ports, and
telecommunications.

2 TRADE AND INVESTMENT REGIMES

2.1 General framework

2.1. During the review period, the structure of Sierra Leone's legal and institutional framework
remained largely unchanged. The current Constitution, originally enacted in 1991, was amended
twice in 2022, primarily to expand the membership of the Electoral Commission and the Political
Parties Regulation Commission.1 Sierra Leone is a democratic unitary State governed by a President
as the Head of State, who is assisted by a Vice President, both of whom are elected by universal
suffrage for a term of five years. Their current term started in 2023 and the next presidential
elections (together with the parliamentary elections) are scheduled for 2028. The Constitution
proclaims the separation of powers between the executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The
structure, functions, and hierarchy of the Government have not undergone any significant change
since 2017.

2.2. In addition to other responsibilities, the President appoints the Cabinet (composed of ministers)
and judges, subject to parliamentary approval. The Cabinet, which includes the Vice President,
assists the President in formulating and implementing policies.

2.3. The unicameral Parliament of Sierra Leone holds legislative power, comprising 149 seats with
direct representation from the district block (majority) and indirect representation from paramount
chiefs (minority). The Cabinet and members of Parliament introduce bills in the Parliament for
enactment. Parliament, therefore, enacts bills with a two-thirds majority vote for presidential assent.
Notably, laws take effect only upon publication in the Official Gazette.

2.4. The hierarchy of laws consists of the Constitution, laws, ordinances, orders, regulations, and
customary laws.2 Domestic laws take precedence over international laws until incorporated into the
national legal system. In effect, international laws can only be applied by the national courts after
they have been adopted into the national system.3

2.5. The Superior Court system in Sierra Leone consists of the Supreme Court, the Court of Appeal,
and the High Court of Justice. The lower courts include the magistrate courts and local courts.
Administratively, there are magistrate courts operating in each district, and each of the chiefdoms
have at least one local court.

2.6. A fast-track commercial court is in place to resolve commercial disputes.

2.7. The authorities are working to enhance the efficiency and integrity of the judicial system
through the Justice Sector Reform Strategy and Investment Policy (JSRSIP), which includes
significant commercial law reforms, such as establishing an efficient alternative dispute resolution
mechanism. During the review period, the 2024-2028 JSRSIP was adopted, replacing the 2015-2018
version.

2.8. In October 2020, Sierra Leone joined the New York Convention on the Recognition and
Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards. In 2022, the English Arbitration Act, 1950, previously in
force under Cap 25 of the Laws of Sierra Leone, 1960, was replaced by a new Arbitration Act based
on the United Nations Convention on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) Model Law.4 The new
legislation regulates both domestic and international arbitrations within the country.5 It provides for
the establishment of the Sierra Leone International Arbitration Centre (SLIAC), a fully independent

2.2 Trade policy formulation and objectives

2.9. The Ministry of Trade and Industry (MTI) remains responsible for formulating trade and trade-
related policies in collaboration with other relevant ministries and agencies, including the Ministry of
Finance and the Ministry of Agriculture, and the private sector.6

2.10. During the review period, Sierra Leone's Medium-Term National Development Plans (MTNDP)
for 2019–2023 and 2024–2030 were the primary socio-economic development frameworks guiding
the country's macroeconomic policies, including trade. Both plans aim to position Sierra Leone as a
middle-income country by 2039, with food security as an immediate priority. The plans seek to
achieve the national development objectives through a green and inclusive economic transformation,
promoting diversification, stimulating growth and employment, and investing in critical infrastructure
to support private sector expansion.7

2.11. The authorities note that Sierra Leone has been unable to fully capitalize on the numerous
opportunities offered by its participation in various preferential trading arrangements, as its exports
remain concentrated in raw and unprocessed metals and precious stones, leaving the economy
largely undiversified. Furthermore, trade has played a marginal role in the country's broader national
development planning. In response, and in alignment with its development strategies, the National
Trade Strategy (NTS) was adopted in 2020, building on the MTNDP 2019–2023's policy focus areas
of economic diversification, infrastructure development, competitiveness, and growth.8 It also aligns
with the MTNDP's macroeconomic goal of reducing the current account deficit from 14% to 11.5%
by promoting exports and encouraging diversification.

2.12. The Strategy emphasizes economic diversification, regional integration, competitiveness, and
inclusivity, with a particular focus on empowering women. To achieve these objectives, the Strategy
is built on four key pillars: (i) engaging with the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA);
(ii) offering trade support services; (iii) promoting industrial growth; and (iv) strengthening
industrial capacities for effective policy development and implementation. While the Strategy
acknowledges Sierra Leone's existing but underutilized preferential market access to the
European Union, the United States, China, the Mano River Union, and Economic Community of West
Africa States (ECOWAS) members, it places special emphasis on actions needed to fully implement
and capitalize on the AfCFTA. The Government plans to raise awareness and take concrete steps to
leverage the opportunities provided by the AfCFTA. Notably, specific recommendations are included
to benefit women entrepreneurs and women-owned businesses in Sierra Leone. In 2021, the Gender
Equality and Women Empowerment Act was adopted. The authorities note that efforts are underway
to enhance the NTS by incorporating key issues such as climate change, support for small and
medium-sized enterprises, and the promotion of women's entrepreneurship.

2.13. As part of the implementation of the NTS, the 2011 National Industrial Policy of Sierra Leone
was revised in 2022 to improve the efficiency of the industrial sector and enhance international
competitiveness. The revised policy aims to create a competitive, sustainable industrial framework
that raises living standards by 2030. It focuses on accelerating industrialization by transforming local
raw materials into finished products, diversifying the industrial base, and strengthening regional and
multilateral cooperation to boost exports of manufactured goods. This initiative has also led to the
revision of the country's Special Economic Zones (SEZs) policy (Section 3).

2.3 Trade agreements and arrangements

2.3.1 WTO

2.14. Sierra Leone has been a WTO Member since 1995 and extends at least MFN treatment to all
its trading partners. Sierra Leone formally accepted the Protocol amending the TRIPS Agreement on
21 March 2017 and deposited its instrument of acceptance of the Trade Facilitation Agreement on
5 May 2017 and of the Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies on 19 July 2024. The country is neither a
party nor an observer to any of the WTO plurilateral agreements.

2.15. Sierra Leone continues to benefit from the WTO's Enhanced Integrated Framework (EIF).
Recent programmes have resulted in the development of the National Tourism Policy (2022) and the
National Export and Trade Facilitation Strategy (2022).

2.16. In negotiations, Sierra Leone remains committed to collaborating with the ACP Group, the
African Group, the G-90, the LDCs, and the "W52". The country currently participates in the Joint
Initiative on Investment Facilitation for Development.

2.17. Sierra Leone's notifications of its trade measures and regulations remain weak. Since 2017,
the country has made notifications in the areas of export subsidies and RTAs.9

2.18. In 2023, the country notified, pursuant to the provisions of paragraph 5 of Article XXVIII of
the GATT 1994, that it reserves the right to modify its goods schedule during the three-year period
commencing on 1 January 2024.10

2.3.2 Regional and preferential agreements

2.3.2.1 African Union (AU) and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

2.19. Sierra Leone, as a founding member of the AU, actively participates in the organization's
initiatives. With the aim to integrate Africa, the AU called for the establishment of Regional Economic
Communities (REC) under the Abuja Treaty. The Treaty, which entered into force in 1994, paved
the way for the establishment of the AfCFTA in 2019 – a flagship project of the AU's Agenda 2063.
The AfCFTA seeks to create a unified market for goods and services, enabling free movement of
persons and investments to enhance Africa's competitiveness, socio-economic development, and
industrial growth.11

2.20. Sierra Leone ratified the Agreement establishing the AfCFTA on 30 April 2019. The AfCFTA
brings together the 55 AU partner States, representing the continent's eight regional economic
communities. The Agreement currently consists of eight protocols, including (i) trade in goods;
(ii) trade in services; (iii) rules and procedures for dispute resolution; (iv) investment; (v) IPRs;
(vi) competition policy; (vii) digital trade; and (viii) women and youth in trade.

2.21. ECOWAS (including Sierra Leone) submitted its common market access offer for goods,
covering 90% of tariff lines, to the AfCFTA Secretariat in December 2020. The offer was adopted by
the AfCFTA Council of Ministers in October 2021, enabling provisional implementation and
preferential trading among State Parties with approved tariff offers.

2.22. Preferential trading under AfCFTA currently occurs through the AfCFTA Secretariat's Guided
Trade Initiative (GTI), launched in June 2022. The GTI aims to match companies and products for
export and import among eligible State Parties, coordinated by their AfCFTA National
Implementation Committees. Sierra Leone is set to participate in the GTI.

2.3.2.2 Economic Community of West Africa States (ECOWAS)

2.24. Sierra Leone has been a member of ECOWAS since its establishment in May 1975. The
community brings together 15 West African countries to achieve economic union by promoting
economic cooperation and integration. To achieve this goal, ECOWAS has established several
protocols to facilitate the free movement of goods, services, capital, and persons and the right of
establishment. These initiatives also focus on creating a common market, harmonizing policies
among member States, and establishing a monetary union.

2.25. As discussed in the previous report, Sierra Leone has been implementing the above-
mentioned protocols through several key initiatives, including the ECOWAS Common External Tariff
(CET), the ECOWAS Trade Liberalization Scheme (ETLS) and the issuance of ECOWAS passports,
and a 90-day waiver of visa and residency permit requirements for community citizens. During the
review period, the bloc adopted a Supplementary Act to amend the ETLS Rules of Origin (RoO),
revising the community's RoO requirements (Section 3.1). Nevertheless, Sierra Leone's official trade
within the bloc remains low, below 0.5% of the total.

2.26. Recent measures to enhance intra-ECOWAS trade facilitation include the establishment by
the Commission, with the support from donors, of an Interconnected System for the Management
of Transit Goods (SIGMAT), which replaced the Inter-State Road Transit and encouraged the
construction of Joint Border Posts (JBPs), including the one between Liberia and Sierra Leone.12

2.27. The single currency goal of the West African Monetary Zone (WAMZ), comprising the Gambia,
Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone, remains pending (Section 1).13

2.3.2.3 Other regional trade agreements

2.28. As discussed in the previous report, Sierra Leone has been a member of the Mano River Union
(MRU) since its establishment in 1973. However, the effective implementation of its regional trade
integration objectives remains a challenge. The MRU consists of Côte d'Ivoire, Guinea, Liberia, and
Sierra Leone, all of which are also members of ECOWAS.14

2.29. Following Sierra Leone's accession to the Community of Sahel-Saharan States (CEN-SAD) in
2008, there has been minimal or no significant progress on trade and the economic integration
process – pending the ratification of the revised CEN-SAD Treaty of 2013. CEN-SAD, which
comprises 25 member States, aims to achieve an economic union through policy harmonization, the
removal of tariff barriers, and the development of infrastructure.15

2.30. The European Union-ECOWAS Economic Partnership Agreement (EU-ECOWAS EPA) has yet
to take effect, pending the signing of the Agreement by all member States and the ratification by
two thirds of the members. The EPA seeks to grant duty-free, quota-free market access to both
parties.

2.31. Sierra Leone is a signatory to various agreements, which aim to enhance economic
development and cooperation between the parties. Only those with the Gambia and Ghana are
currently active.

2.4 Investment regime

2.35. Since 2017, efforts have been made to reform Sierra Leone's investment climate and
regulatory framework to attract foreign direct investment (FDI), including the establishment of the
National Investment Board (NIB) in 2022, following the enactment of the National Investment Board
Act, 2022. The Board is mandated to serve as a one-stop shop to streamline investment facilitation
processes as well as coordinate and promote investment activities in the country. The NIB's
responsibilities span facilitating partnerships, evaluating investment initiatives, issuing incentive
certificates, and providing institutional support to investors, among others.17

2.36. The National Investment Board Act, 2022 repealed the Investment Promotion Act, 2004 and
the Sierra Leone Investment and Export Promotion Agency Act, 2007. With a view to reshuffling the
institutional framework, the Public-Private Partnership Act, 2014, the Companies Act, 2009, and the
Registration of Business Act, 2007 were amended, replacing the Public-Private Partnership Unit, the
Corporate Affairs Commission, and the Sierra Leone Investment and Export Promotion Agency with
specific directorates under the NIB.

2.37. The NIB acts as a one-stop shop for business setup, offering support and information for
starting and running businesses. Its Business Facilitation Directorate handles and forwards
applications for registration, permits, licences, and clearances to relevant ministries and agencies.18
Meanwhile, the Corporate Affairs Directorate oversees the incorporation and registration of
companies.

2.38. While the broad structure of incentives available to investors has remained largely unchanged
since 2017, the Tax and Duty Concession Act, 2022 has introduced renewed discipline in the granting
of tax incentives (Section 3.3.1). The incentives offered (ranging from general and sector-specific
tax concessions to customs duty exemptions and various non-tax benefits) are designed to stimulate
development projects and support the establishment of new enterprises.19 The annual Finance Acts
regularly modify tax concessions for some industries. Among recent such measures, the Finance
Act, 2024 provides duty-free importation of plants, machinery, and equipment for manufacturing
biodegradable plastic alternatives for five years, in accordance with the Tax and Duty Exemption
Act, 2023.20

2.5 Regulatory framework

2.43. Sierra Leone is generally open to foreign investments in most economic sectors. However,
restrictions remain for activities such as foreign investments in mining valued at USD 500,000 or
less, which require a 25% national shareholding.

2.44. Restrictions on land ownership for foreign investors remain in effect. Foreigners are allowed
to lease land for up to 50 years, with the possibility of a 21-year extension, contingent upon the
territorial land governance system. Alternatively, leases of up to 99 years are permitted for mining
activities.

2.45. In 2023, Sierra Leone adopted the establishment of the Koya Industrial Zone, a new area
created through a public-private partnership aimed at enhancing FDI. The industrial zone is set to
offer essential infrastructure, including transport, logistics, and energy systems, to support industrial
activities. It is also expected to streamline procedures for business permits and approvals. At its
initial stage, the zone's focus will be on import substitution through the local manufacturing of tiles,
processing of iron ore for construction, and the creation of pharmaceutical products. In agriculture,
it aims to enhance "value capture by producing finished and packaged goods from forestry, cotton,
soya, and cashew".23 The authorities indicate that an SEZ policy was recently adopted to regulate
the creation of various industrial zones.

2.6 Investment cooperation

2.46. Sierra Leone is a member of the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency, a signatory to
the 1958 New York Convention on Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, and a
party to the ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes) Convention.

2.47. Sierra Leone has bilateral investment treaties (BITs) with Germany (signed in 1965) and the
United Kingdom (signed in 1981 and revised in 2000). It has also signed treaties that are not yet in
force with China and the United Arab Emirates. 24 Sierra Leone also has double taxation agreements
with Canada, Denmark, the Gambia, Ghana, New Zealand, Nigeria, Norway, South Africa, and the
United Kingdom.

3.1 Measures directly affecting imports

3.1.1 Customs procedures, valuation, and requirements

3.1. Companies importing goods into Sierra Leone must be incorporated with the Corporate Affairs
Commission and registered with the Office of the Administrator and Registrar General (OARG).
Additionally, importers are required to obtain a taxpayer identification number (TIN) from the
National Revenue Authority (NRA) to clear goods through customs. These requirements apply
equally to both domestic and foreign entities.

3.2. The legal framework governing customs issues in Sierra Leone remained largely unchanged
during the review period. The Customs Act, 2011 (last amended in 2024) continues to regulate
customs procedures, with its administration overseen by the Customs and Excise Department (CED)
of the NRA. The NRA maintains a website where information on import and export procedures can
be found (www.nra.gov.sl).

3.3. Several other international instruments also shape customs procedures. For example,
Sierra Leone is a member of the World Customs Organization and a contracting party to the
Kyoto Convention. On 5 May 2017, the country ratified the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement and
notified the WTO of the list of measures under Categories A, B, and C on 22 February 2018.1

3.4. Customs procedures in Sierra Leone are influenced by several factors that can increase trading
costs for businesses, including role duplication and the application of various fees. For instance,
imported goods are subject to a destination inspection scheme managed by a single company,
Integrated Trade Services (ITS). This scheme assesses the quality, quantity, value, and customs
classification of imports prior to clearance, which are tasks traditionally performed by customs
authorities. The inspection company provides recommendations on classification and valuation for
Customs' review and charges a service fee of 1% of the assessed f.o.b. value of each consignment.
Furthermore, customs authorities often disagree with the classifications or valuations provided by
the inspectors and make adjustments. In 2024, approximately 80% of the inspectors' conclusions
were accepted by Customs, and the remaining 20% were subject to changes.

3.5. The following items are exempt from the destination inspection scheme: arms, ammunition,
weapons, implements of war, gold and other precious stones, explosives and pyrotechnic products,
live animals, scrap metals, crude oil, personal and household effects, supplies for non-governmental
organizations and diplomats for their own use, and frozen fish caught by vessels owned by
Sierra Leoneans.

3.6. Customs declaration formalities may be conducted by a trader or a customs broker. They must
be approved by the Commissioner-General of the NRA and should have the ability to use ASYCUDA,
be trained in customs procedures, and provide a SLE 20,000 guaranteed bond. A list of licensed
customs agents is published in the Gazette.

3.7. In general, customs declarations are done electronically. For all imports, a Single Custom
Declaration must be submitted with accompanying documentation, which includes the invoice, the
Classification and Valuation Certificate (CVC) delivered by the destination inspection company, the
delivery order, the packing list, the original copy of the Bill of Lading, and any applicable certificates
(e.g. sanitary, phytosanitary, origin) or any other documents required by specific laws.

3.8. The NRA upgraded from ASYCUDA++ to ASYCUDA World in January 2019 and introduced the
Electronic Single Window in 2020. According to the authorities, the single window provides for the
automation of procedures with external stakeholders (SPS and standards-setting authorities). For
example, the automation of the duty waiver process allows all involved government agencies to
recommend, approve, and endorse as their function requires.

3.9. The customs clearance process in Sierra Leone utilizes a risk management system integrated
within ASYCUDA World. This system, managed by a specialized Risk Management Unit, assigns
consignments to one of four selectivity lanes based on assessed risk levels: green for low-risk goods,
enabling immediate release without additional checks; yellow, requiring a documentary review; red,
involving both documentary checks and physical examinations; and blue, reserved for perishable
goods, which are subject to post-clearance audits.

3.10. While the system is designed to prioritize efficiency and focus resources on high-risk
consignments, its implementation has encountered challenges. According to the authorities, a
significant proportion of customs declarations are processed through the red lane, indicating a higher
frequency of physical inspections than originally intended. Furthermore, the green lane is
underutilized for broader trade facilitation. This misalignment undermines the core objective of the
risk management system: to expedite clearance for low-risk consignments while concentrating
scrutiny on high-risk goods.

3.11. The authorities indicate that several trade facilitation reforms are ongoing, with substantial
progress made towards their completion. These reforms include the adoption of a legal framework
for the implementation of an Authorized Economic Operator programme and the conducting of a
time release study. The aim is to identify potential corrective actions for Customs and other border
agencies to enhance their performance.

3.12. For certain trade facilitation reforms that have already been implemented, it is essential to
raise awareness within the business community. For instance, a binding advance ruling mechanism
is in place with an emphasis on tariff classification, determination of origin, and the valuation
method. However, it remains significantly underutilized, with the authorities reporting that only
about 1% of traders currently make use of this tool.

3.13. Sierra Leone's rules on customs valuation are found in the Customs Act, 2011, which is, in
principle, aligned with the WTO Agreement on Customs Valuation. Customs duties are determined
based on goods' c.i.f. value.

3.14. As was the case during the previous Review, Customs have the authority to examine and
redetermine the classification, value, or country of origin of goods within five years of the date of
the customs declaration. If such a decision is made, it can be appealed to the Commissioner-General
within 90 days of receiving the formal notice. However, filing an appeal does not suspend the
payment of the reassessed import duties and taxes by the importer. The Act mandates the
Commissioner-General to address the appeal promptly but does not specify a timeframe. Should the
importer be dissatisfied with the Commissioner-General's decision, it may further appeal to the High
Court within 90 days of the notification of the decision.

3.15. A dispute resolution system is available for all customs-related matters, which can be
addressed through the Objections and Appeals Committee of the NRA, with a representative from
Customs. The authorities note that importers have been informed about their right to appeal if they
disagree with a customs re-determination. However, it appears that, to date, no importer has utilized
this dispute resolution process for customs issues.

3.1.2 Rules of origin

3.16. Sierra Leone implements both non-preferential and preferential rules of origin (RoOs).

3.17. Under the Customs Act, 2011, non-preferential RoOs are in place with a view to applying
quantitative restrictions or other trade measures. The rules determine the country of origin of a
product based on two main criteria:

      •   Wholly obtained: the product is considered to originate from the country where it was
          entirely obtained or produced; or

      •   Substantial transformation: if a product is not wholly obtained, its country of origin is
          determined by the location of its last substantial transformation, defined as a change in
          the product's tariff classification (heading or subheading). ECOWAS' relevant protocols can
          also be used in assigning a specific origin to a product.

3.18. According to the ECOWAS Protocol, goods are considered to originate from a member State
if they are wholly produced within that State or have undergone sufficient transformation. In 2018,
the ECOWAS RoOs were amended, including exceptions to the sufficient transformation
requirement.2 The relevant implementation regulations were adopted in 2021. A product is now
considered sufficiently transformed if there is a change in tariff heading or if non-originating
materials do not exceed 68% of the ex-works price of the product (previously set at 70%).

3.19. Under the ECOWAS Trade Liberalization Scheme (ETLS), industrial goods must undergo a dual
approval process to be recognized as originating ECOWAS products. First, designated authorities in
the exporting country must approve the firm as a producer of the goods within a member State (firm
eligibility).3 Second, the product itself must be approved based on origin criteria (product
admissibility). This dual approval requirement does not apply to agricultural, livestock, forestry, or
traditional craft products.

3.20. The RoOs under the yet-to-be-implemented African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) are
designed to harmonize RoOs across various free trade agreements in Africa. The AfCFTA Agreement
stipulates product-specific RoOs that determine the origin of goods based on whether they are wholly
obtained or sufficiently worked or processed, using one of five criteria: value addition, non-
originating material content, change in tariff heading, specific processes, and specific rules.4 As of
June 2024, product-specific RoOs had been agreed upon for about 92% of tariff lines. Once
implemented, AfCFTA RoOs are set to allow full cumulation among members.

3.1.3 Tariffs

3.1.3.1 Applied MFN tariff

3.21. Sierra Leone has implemented the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (CET) since 2022. During
a transitional period ending in January 2028, ECOWAS member States have the option to apply an
Import Adjustment Tax (IAT) on goods imported from non-ECOWAS countries. This measure allows
for adjustments in tariff protection to mitigate the impact of the ECOWAS CET. However, the tariff
lines subject to the IAT are limited to a maximum of 3% of the total tariff lines.

3.22. In September 2024, using HS22 classification for 6,381 tariff lines at 10-digit tariff line level,
the average unweighted applied MFN tariff rate was 11.8%, down from 12% in 2016 (Table 3.1). As
at the time of the previous Review, with a coefficient of variation of 0.6%, rates are moderately
dispersed around the average. Agricultural products (by the WTO definition) continue to attract
higher rates (14.4% on average), but they registered a relatively more significant reduction during
the review period (the average was 15.6% during the previous Review). The average tariff rate on
non-agricultural products stood at 11.3%, a slight decrease from 11.4% in 2016. Highest rates apply
to dairy products, beverages and tobacco, and textiles and clothing.

3.24.   The applied tariff undergoes regular reviews, primarily through finance acts. For example, the
Finance Act, 2024 sets new customs duty rates for cement (20%), rice (5%), iron rod (10%), and
cooking gas (5%)5, while the Finance Act, 2022 had previously set rates for cement at 10%, iron
rod at 5%, and rice and cooking gas at zero. These frequent revisions affect the stability and
predictability of the tariff regime, which can hamper business confidence in the domestic economy.
Sierra Leone does not administer any tariff quotas.

3.27. Under the ECOWAS ETLS, Sierra Leone provides duty-free access to products that are
approved and produced by certified enterprises located in any ECOWAS member State (Section 2).

3.1.3.4 Duty concessions and exemptions

3.28. Duty concessions and exemptions are generally granted in relation to economic incentive
schemes (Section 3.1.1), under ratified international agreements (e.g. the Vienna Convention),
pursuant to special customs regimes, or under bilateral agreements with certain companies, in
particular in the mining sector.

3.29. The World Bank notes that tax waivers granted to organizations, entities, and individuals,
along with tax exemptions under key tax instruments in Sierra Leone, resulted in an annual tax
revenue loss of 6% during the 2009-19 period.6 Exemptions granted through discretionary decisions
on an ad hoc basis have played a significant role in exacerbating fiscal expenditure. During the
previous Review, it was indicated that the Finance Act, 2013 introduced a cap of 3% of GDP on duty
waivers granted in a given year. However, it appears that no formal evaluation of this policy was
conducted during the review period.

3.30. In 2023, the Tax and Duty Exemption Act was adopted.7 It seeks to establish a clear legal
and regulatory framework to streamline and govern the process of granting duty and tax
exemptions.

3.31. The Act recognizes the importance of promoting national economic and social policy goals
through tax exemptions. Additionally, it aims to streamline current exemptions and privileges,
introduce sanctions to limit discretion and prevent potential abuse, and, where necessary,
consolidate existing statutory provisions on exemptions to improve administration and boost
domestic revenue. The authorities believe that this initiative will help the Government achieve its
goal of increasing domestic revenue mobilization.8

3.32. Duty-free importation is granted to items such as agricultural inputs and energy and in the
context of infrastructure projects.

3.33. Exemption from customs duties may be granted for imported goods designated for inward
processing. In addition, goods re-imported into Sierra Leone in the same condition within one year
of their export may qualify for exemption from import duties and taxes.

3.34. Section 51 of the Customs Act stipulates that access to a Special Economic Zone (SEZ) shall
be granted for imported goods without requiring payment of customs duty or Goods and Services
Tax (GST). The exemption applies to all goods directly used in the production of export goods from
Sierra Leone within the SEZ by licensed enterprises. In practice, the authorities indicate that no duty
or tax concession had been planned in the context of the newly established SEZ.

3.1.4 Other charges affecting imports

3.1.4.1 Goods and Services Tax

3.35. In accordance with the Goods and Services Tax Act, 2009, Sierra Leone maintains a GST on
a list of domestically produced and imported goods. The main adjustment to the GST regime during
the review period was the amendment introduced by the Finance Act, 2021. In response to the
growing significance of the digital economy, this amendment updated the GST Tax Act to include
digital market supplies and digital services as taxable transactions.9 Additionally, the Finance Act,
2023 further expanded the scope of this provision to cover a wider range of digital services. 10

3.36. The GST is levied at a standard rate of 15%. Exports are zero-rated. On imports, the base
comprises the c.i.f. value, customs duties, and any other tax or fiscal charge payable on the
importation of goods.

3.37. GST-exempted goods and services include animals; fish and birds imported for breeding and
rearing purposes; agricultural inputs, seeds, bulbs, and roots imported for propagation; rice (in its
raw state); machinery for use in agriculture, veterinary, fishing, horticulture, manufacturing, and
mining; exports of minerals; water (excluding package and distilled water); crude oil and
hydrocarbon products (excluding lubricating oils); books and newspapers; land, buildings, and public
work; and some services (education, medical, and pharmaceuticals services; passenger
transportation; and financial services).

3.1.4.2 Excise duties

3.38. Sierra Leone levies excise duties, primarily in accordance with the Excise Act, 1982. Excise
duties apply to selected items at specific rates and at ad valorem rates between 5% and 20%. The
list comprises gambling services; non-alcoholic beverages; alcoholic beverages such as beer and
wine; tobacco products, including e-cigarettes; petroleum products; and certain plastic containers.
The tax base is determined by the c.i.f. value for imports or the sale value for domestic goods. Tax
rates are periodically updated through finance acts, with the latest adjustments outlined in the
Finance Act, 2024.11 Domestically manufactured beverages generally attract lower rates than
imported ones.

3.1.4.3 Other duties and charges

3.39. Imports from non-ECOWAS countries are subject to the ECOWAS Community Levy at the rate
of 0.5% of the c.i.f. value from non-ECOWAS countries. The proceeds from the levy aim to finance
the operating expenses of the ECOWAS Commission and regional integration projects.

3.40. During the previous Review, it was indicated that bills of lading for duty-free imports are
subject to a stamp duty of SLE 750. Port charges are assessed on the volume of imports and exports,
at rates ranging from SLE 0.5 tonne to SLE 1 tonne. A customs processing fee of SLE 30 is applicable
to all transactions. However, updated information was not available during the current review. A
stamp duty is levied at 20% on imported and domestically produced tobacco products and alcoholic
beverages.

3.41. A withholding tax (collected as a prepayment of the income tax) is charged at a rate of 3%
of the c.i.f. value of imported goods intended for resale.

with the Customs Act, 2011, import control measures aim to protect, inter alia, public morality;
human, animal, plant life or health; or the environment. Prohibitions and restrictions can also be

3.43. The importation of narcotic drugs is strictly prohibited. The National Drugs Control Act, 2008

3.44. As a signatory to the Montreal Protocol, Sierra Leone prohibits the import and export of

3.1.6 Anti-dumping, countervailing, and safeguard measures

3.47. Sierra Leone does not have a legislative or institutional framework for anti-dumping,
countervailing, or safeguard actions and has not implemented any measures related to these areas.
In 2014, it notified to the WTO that it does not have legislation, regulation, or administrative
procedures on safeguard measures.13

3.48. Under the ECOWAS tariff regime, the supplementary protection tax acts as a safeguard
mechanism aimed at protecting local products. It can be enforced if the average import volume of a
product into the customs territory of a member State increases by at least 25% over the past
three years for which data are available. The tax may also be applied if, during a specific month, the
average c.i.f. import price of a product (in national currency) falls below 80% of the average c.i.f.
price over the previous three years. Depending on the circumstances, the tax can be imposed for a
maximum period of one or two years. The authorities indicate that Sierra Leone has never used the
supplementary protection tax.

3.2 Measures directly affecting exports

3.2.1 Customs procedures and requirements

3.49. The registration formalities applicable to importers of goods for commercial purposes,
including customs procedures, generally also apply to exporters (Section 3.1).

3.50. The Chamber of Commerce, Industry, and Agriculture provides certificates of origin and
certifies other export documents for preferential exports to the United States, ECOWAS member
States, and the European Union.

3.51. Additionally, diamond exporters are required to obtain a licence from the Ministry of Mineral
Resources. The Government Gold and Diamond Office (GGDO) is responsible for certifying all legally
exported diamonds from Sierra Leone.

3.52. As at the time of the previous Review, all exports of coffee, cocoa, palm kernel oil, palm kernel
cake, ginger, and piassava require an export permit from the Ministry of Trade and Industry, which
issues the licence only if the Produce Monitoring Board (PMB) inspects the exporter's premises to
certify compliance with statutory requirements and ensure that the products meet international
standards.

3.53. Exporters must submit the following documents along with their customs declaration: a valid
business registration or incorporation certificate, a tax clearance certificate, and (for agricultural
products) a letter from the PMB confirming that the exporter has fulfilled the statutory requirements
by maintaining an appropriate store equipped with all necessary instruments for operation.

3.54. Exporters are required to repatriate at least 30% of the export proceeds through the
commercial banking system within 90 days from the date of export.

3.2.2 Taxes, charges, and levies

3.55. Taxes, charges, and levies on exports in Sierra Leone have remained broadly unchanged since
the previous Review, continuing to significantly increase the country's trading costs and affect its
competitiveness in the global market. For instance, exporters are required to pay an inspection fee
of 1% of the f.o.b. value of exports, imposed by ITS as part of the quality assessment process. This
leads to redundancy, as exports undergo further assessments upon arrival at their destination,
increasing the financial strain on exporters.

3.56. Additionally, exporters of coffee, cocoa, palm kernel oil, palm kernel cake, ginger, and
piassava are required to pay a 2.5% levy on the f.o.b. value of their exports.

3.57. An export tax of 3% applies to diamonds. Small-scale mining leaseholders are subject to a
5% royalty fee based on the export value, along with a 1% valuation fee. Individuals exporting on
an occasional basis can obtain a special exemption from the licensing requirement but will incur an
additional 2% charge on top of the 3% export tax.

3.58. Gold exports are taxed at 5% of their assessed value by the Precious Minerals Trading
Directorate. However, individually mined gold benefits from a reduced tax rate of 3%.

3.59. In general, exports are zero-rated for GST. However, exports of certain minerals, including
rutile and its byproducts, iron ore, bauxite, gold, and diamonds, are taxed at the standard rate of
15%.

3.2.3 Export prohibitions, restrictions, and licensing

3.60. Similar to import regulations, export control measures are designed to protect public health,
ensure safety, and promote environmental sustainability. Most of these measures are aligned with
international agreements to which Sierra Leone is a signatory. For instance, under the
Montreal Protocol, the export of ozone-depleting substances is strictly prohibited. Additionally, the
National Drugs Control Act, 2008 restricts the export of certain drugs. Sierra Leone has also
periodically imposed export bans on logs to mitigate environmental harm. The authorities also note
that temporary bans also applied to palm oil, rice, and scrap metal during the review period.

3.61. For environmental purposes, exports of plants and charcoal require a special permit from the
ministry in charge of natural resources.

3.62. Permits are also required for the exportation of gold, diamonds, and a list of agricultural
products (Section 3.2.1).

3.2.4 Export support and promotion

3.63. Sierra Leone has never submitted a subsidy notification to the WTO Committee on Subsidies
and Countervailing Measures. In its most recent notification in 2023, it notified the Committee on
Agriculture that no export subsidies were granted to agricultural products in 202114, consistent with
its practice in previous years throughout the review period.

3.64. Throughout most of the review period, the Sierra Leone Investment and Export Promotion
Agency (SLIEPA) was responsible for promoting and encouraging the development of
export-oriented activities. Its role included providing exporters with market access information and
technical assistance opportunities, as well as advocating on their behalf. Key activities involved

3.67. Sierra Leone does not have an export processing zone in place and encounters difficulties in
implementing an effective export promotion strategy. The authorities note that the lack of
collaboration among the various public agencies responsible for export-related matters constitutes
one of the main barriers to export development.

3.2.5 Export finance, insurance, and guarantees

3.68. In general, access to finance is challenging for Sierra Leone. The country does not maintain
a public mechanism to support or facilitate export financing.

3.69. In the current context, commercial banks are the sole providers of trade finance instruments.
Some banks note that certain regulatory tools from the Bank of Sierra Leone (BSL) hinder their
ability to provide guarantees. For instance, when assessing the risk profile of a commercial bank,
the BSL treats a loan it guarantees as a fully-fledged loan, which discourages banks from engaging
in loan guarantees for exporters.

3.3 Measures affecting production and trade

3.3.1 Incentives

3.70. Exemptions and reductions in border taxes (Section 3.1.3.4) and income tax remain the
primary mechanisms for providing incentives to businesses in Sierra Leone. Companies in
Sierra Leone are subject to a corporate income tax rate of 25%. Manufacturing entities located
outside the Western area enjoy a reduced rate of 15%. Additionally, all companies, regardless of
their performance, are liable for a minimum alternative tax rate of 2% on turnover (down from 3%
in 2023).

3.71. Sierra Leone offers a range of incentives to promote private sector investment, including
exemptions from income tax, deductions for tax purposes, and exemptions from import duties and
GST. These incentives are subject to meeting certain criteria, such as enhancing local content
(Section 2).

3.72. General incentives available include income tax relief on plant, machinery, and equipment; a
three-year exemption from import duties for businesses importing these items; reduced import duty
rates on raw materials; and 100% tax deductions for expenses related to research and development,
employee training, and social service development. Additionally, sector-specific incentive
programmes are offered to encourage investments in agriculture, energy, infrastructure, tourism,
and pharmaceuticals (Table A3.1).

3.73. Incentives are also offered to SEZ companies. These include three-year corporate tax holidays
and streamlined government services, such as expedited customs, immigration, and registration
processes.

3.3.2 Standards and other technical requirements

3.74. The legal and institutional framework for standards and technical regulations remained
broadly unchanged during the review period.

3.75. The Standards Act No. 2 of 1996 continues to serve as the legal framework for regulating
standardization, certification, and conformity assessment. This Act also led to the establishment of
the Sierra Leone Standards Bureau (SLSB), which is responsible for overseeing standardization and
quality management activities. It further established the National Standards Council, which was
operationalized in 2020 and is tasked with approving the standardization work programme and the
list of technical committees. The Council's membership includes representatives from various
ministries, such as those overseeing agriculture, health, and the environment, along with members
from academic institutions and business associations.

3.76. As of January 2025, despite the existence of several technical regulations, Sierra Leone has
not notified any technical regulations or designated a national enquiry point under the
WTO Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT Agreement). However, in the context of this
review, the authorities indicate that the SLSB has been assigned the role of the country's enquiry
point under the TBT Agreement.

3.77. Additionally, in accordance with the Weight and Measures Act No. 5 of 2010, the SLSB is
responsible for the enforcement of the metric system of measurement.

3.78. The development of standards is driven by technical committees responsible for specific
subjects. These committees include representatives from academia, researchers, conformity
assessment bodies, regulators, industry, trade associations, and consumer advocacy groups. They
collaborate to develop national standards aligned with market needs. The SLSB serves as the
secretariat and project manager for these committees. The authorities note that SLSB has adopted
the relevant ISO/IEC Directives for its standardization process.

3.79. At the time of the current Review, the SLSB manages technical committees covering areas
such as water quality, photovoltaic/renewable energy, electrical and electronics, mechanical
engineering, civil engineering, energy management, information security management systems, and
food and agriculture.16 This represents an expansion in the number of committees and a better
specialization compared with the situation at the time of the previous Review, where the SLSB
managed only five committees: the National Codex Committee, the Industrial Standards Board, the
Metrology Committee, the Food and Agriculture Committee, and the Engineering Technical
Committee.

3.80. The standards development process begins by assessing the country's needs and priorities
and, through consultations with relevant stakeholders, drafting the SLSB standards work
programme. Standards are then developed by the appropriate technical committees and published
in the Gazette. These committees often base their work on international standards, including those
from regional organizations. In cases where no international standards exist, such as for indigenous
products, the SLSB creates its own guidelines. Recent efforts have focused on aligning with regional
frameworks like the ECOWAS Quality Policy (ECOQUAL) to harmonize standards across West Africa.
As of September 2024, the SLSB has published 349 nationally developed and internationally adapted
standards.17

3.81. Once a standard is published in the Gazette, the relevant industry regulator may present it to
Parliament for approval to make it mandatory. A 60-day public comment period is required before
the standard can be adopted as a technical regulation. Standards are usually made technical
regulations at the request of sectoral ministers. The authorities note that as of January 2025, the
technical regulations in force in Sierra Leone include 15 regional harmonized technical regulations
(ECOWAS level) and one national technical regulation.

formerly known as the American Society for Testing and Materials. Under the agreement, the SLSB

3.3.3 Marking, labelling, and packaging

3.84. Products must be labelled in English to show basic information regarding name, weight,
manufacture dates, expiration dates, ingredients, and origin.

3.85. The Weights and Measures Act, 2010 mandates that sellers of packaged goods display both
the gross and net weights or measurements on the packaging. For canned goods, food items, and
pharmaceuticals, the net weight or quantity of contents, along with the country of origin, must also
be clearly indicated. Additionally, the Act requires the liquid volume of beverages and the yardage
and width of textiles to be properly labelled.

3.3.4 Sanitary and phytosanitary requirements

3.86. Sierra Leone is a member of the Codex Alimentarius Commission, the World Organisation for
Animal Health, and the International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC).18

3.87. The country did not notify any SPS regulations during the review period. It has also not
notified the WTO of its sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) legislation or designated an enquiry point
under the SPS Agreement. However, the authorities have stated that the National SPS Committee
serves as the established enquiry point, and the SLSB (under the Ministry of Trade and Industry)
functions as the notification authority.

3.88. The main SPS provisions related to trade in plant products are outlined in the Agriculture
Inspection Rules under Chapter 185 (Cap 185) of 1946 for exports and the Plant Phytosanitary
(Import) Rules of 1974 (amended in 1976). The latter lists 64 categories of plants and plant
products, specifying which are prohibited from import unless accompanied by a phytosanitary
certificate, inspection, or treatment. In the context of this Review, the authorities noted in 2024 that
an updated pest list is under preparation. The Food and Feed Safety Act, adopted in 2017 in
Sierra Leone, aims to ensure the safety and quality of food and feed, protect public health, promote
fair trade practices, and address related matters.19 According to the authorities, other sources for
standards and requirements regarding the import of food products, animals, and plants are the Trade
and Food Standards set by the FAO and WHO, the WHO's International Health Regulations (Third
Edition (2005)), the EU Legal Requirements for Imports of Fruits and Vegetables (A Supplier's
Guide), and the Codex Alimentarius.

3.89. In general, the SPS regulatory framework for imports and exports of agricultural products is
outdated and misaligned with modern agricultural practices and priority products. For example,
various international organizations, including the International Trade Centre and the United Nations
Industrial Development Organization note that since Cap 185 was enacted in 1946, quality standards
have evolved, leading to discrepancies between Cap 185 and international best practices.20 This
misalignment creates technical barriers to trade. Modernizing the framework could encourage

3.91. The Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Food Security (MAFFS) holds the primary

Ministry of Health and Sanitation is responsible for enforcing SPS regulations concerning food safety
and pharmaceuticals. In addition, the Fishery Products Regulations, 2007 designate the Food Unit

compliance of fishery and aquaculture products with the regulations.

3.92. The institutional framework has been under construction over the last couple of years, but
progress was slow during the review period. In 2017, the Food and Feed Safety Act provided for the
establishment of an Authority tasked with developing and maintaining food and feed safety control
systems. Upon its establishment, the Authority will be expected to regulate food and feed businesses
and ensure compliance with prescribed standards throughout the food and feed chain. The Act also
called for the creation of a Food and Feed Safety Scientific Committee to provide scientific advice,
technical support, and policy recommendations. The Committee is also expected to be responsible
for delivering independent information and communicating risks.

3.93. The Crop Protection Unit under MAFFS is responsible for conducting phytosanitary inspections
and certifications for plant imports. However, due to a lack of designated laboratories, inspections
are only physical. The PMB, established by an Act of Parliament in 2013, is tasked with regulating
export quality control for agricultural products in line with the Agriculture Inspection Rules under
Chapter 185 (Cap 185).22 The Livestock and Animal Health Department of MAFFS conducts
inspections of livestock, which were limited to two border posts at the time of the previous Review.
Inspection fees are borne by importers and exporters. However, the authorities did not provide
information about applicable rates.

3.94. In general, a phytosanitary certificate is required for the international transport of plant
materials and products. For exports, these certificates are issued by the Phytosanitary Control Unit
within MAFFS in accordance with the IPPC format. Certificates are issued following a pest-risk
analysis for plant products considered "pathways" for pest introduction, which require fumigation.

3.95. Sierra Leone participates in regional SPS frameworks to further reinforce its biosafety
framework. For instance, the Revised ECOWAS Treaty encourages the harmonization of plant and
animal protection measures, as well as agricultural strategies and regulations within West Africa.
Furthermore, the African Union introduced the SPS Policy Framework for Africa in 2019 to promote
enhanced cooperation and harmonization of SPS measures across the continent.23 However,
it appears that the lack of assessment mechanisms to monitor member States' SPS measures has
continued to hinder the effective implementation of regional regulations.

3.3.6 State trading and state-owned enterprises

3.100. Sierra Leone has not submitted any notifications to the WTO regarding state trading
activities as defined under Article XVII:4(a) of the GATT 1994.

3.101. The National Commission for Privatization (NCP) is the governmental body responsible for
overseeing the management, privatization, and reform of public enterprises, as outlined in the
NCP Act, 2002. The Commission is authorized to approve divestiture policies, establish guidelines
for the valuation of public enterprises being privatized, and select strategic investors. However,
the Finance Act, 2020 transferred full oversight of Sierra Leone Commercial Bank and Rokel
Commercial Bank to the Ministry of Finance.

3.103. In 2022, a State Ownership and Governance Policy for State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs) was
adopted to address the inefficiencies within SOEs and mitigate their fiscal impact.25 The Policy
stipulates that SOEs' operations should be justified only when necessary to correct market failures,
manage strategic national assets, or implement public policy objectives. It acknowledges that poor
oversight and governance have contributed to SOEs' underperformance, leading to fiscal risks for
Sierra Leone and inadequate service delivery. As of December 2019, SOEs' contingent liabilities were
estimated at USD 285.82 million, representing 6.8% of the country's GDP. More recent information

3.104. Recognizing the need for improved SOE performance, the Policy focuses on strengthening
oversight and governance. It proposes the repeal of the NCP Act, 2002 and the enactment of new
legislation to establish a centralized ownership entity, accountable to the Office of the President, to

competitive markets do not receive preferential treatment over private sector companies. This
includes SOEs being required to pay all applicable taxes and fees, without gaining special access to
resources, services, or financing unavailable to the private sector. The authorities indicate that a
new bill has been initiated to further improve the management of SOEs. It is also expected to further
clarify the oversight of SOEs.

3.3.7 Government procurement

3.105. The primary goal of Sierra Leone's public procurement system is to ensure that government
funds are utilized transparently, efficiently, and equitably.26 In accordance with the legislation in
place and as a way of supporting domestic suppliers and the development of domestically produced
goods, a procuring entity may provide a margin of preference to favour bids from domestic
contractors. The Finance Act, 2019 provided for the granting of preferential measures by procuring
entities at the district level.

3.106. In addition, Sierra Leone's public procurement rules and standards encourage procuring
entities to consider environmental factors during bid preparation and evaluation, including the
prevention or minimization of waste and pollution, energy conservation, and promotion of
environmentally friendly products. However, the authorities note that such standards are not yet
implemented.

3.107. In an effort to enhance the efficiency of the procurement process, the NPPA is planning to
implement an electronic procurement system for government entities in 2025.

3.108. According to the authorities, the total value of procurement in 2021 stood at SLE 1.9 billion.

3.109. An annual public procurement report is, in principle, published every year to present the
main developments, including challenges faced by the overall system.27

3.115. The Act requires that all public procurement be carried out through advertised open bidding
processes, ensuring equal access for all eligible and qualified bidders without discrimination.

3.116. To be eligible, bidders must provide documentary evidence certifying their compliance with
prescribed requirements. The required documentation includes certificates of registration, tax
documents, declarations regarding conflict of interest and criminal history, and evidence of
qualifications, financial resources, and past performance, among other specified criteria. The legal
framework mandates that the procuring entity must not unfairly discriminate against foreign bidders
when establishing the required documentation. SOEs can participate in public procurement if they
are legally and financially autonomous, operates under commercial law, and are not a dependent
agency of the procuring entity.

3.117. Procurement units must use the appropriate procurement method, taking into account the
specific requirements and the revised thresholds established in the 2020 Regulations. Subject to,
inter alia, available qualified local bidders, a national bidding procedure must be used for
procurements below SLE 5 million for goods and services and SLE 8 million for works. An
international competitive bidding procedure is required for procurements above these thresholds.
Restricted bidding procedures can be used when the products are available from a limited pool of
suppliers, or when the time and cost associated with evaluating a large number of bids are
considered disproportionate to the procurement's value.

3.118. Requests for quotations from at least three bidders must be used for procurement of goods
and services below SLE 250,000 and procurements of works below SLE 500,000. Sole-source
procurement (direct contracting) is allowed only under specific and restricted conditions, such as
when the works or services can only be provided by a single source due to physical, technical, or
policy constraints or in emergency situations. In all instances, approval from the NPPA is mandatory.
Request for proposals is the preferred method in the case of procurement of consultant services.
A solicitation for expressions of interest is required when the estimated value of the consultants'
services is above SLE 5 million.

3.119. Apart from the thresholds, factors to be considered in deciding the procurement method to
be used remained broadly unchanged during the review period. Any proposed change to a
procurement method must be justified in writing by the procurement unit and receive approval from
the Procurement Committee.

3.120. The Act prohibits procuring entities to divide procurements into lots for the purpose of
avoiding contract approval thresholds. According to its 2019 report (the most recent report
available), the NPPA notes that although there was progress in the value of procurements conducted
through open competitive methods, several entities continue to intentionally split procurement
activities to bypass open competitive processes persists.30 In the period 2018-21, request for
quotation was the most used procurement method by number, while competitive bidding was the
most used method by value (Table A3.2). The significant scale of government procurement in
Sierra Leone, coupled with the widespread use of manoeuvres to bypass established competitive
and transparent procurement procedures, highlights persistent public financial challenges that
continue to impede the efficient allocation of resources in the economy.

3.121. The contract must be awarded to the bidder with the lowest evaluated and substantially
responsive bid that meets the specified evaluation criteria. Contract awards must be published when
the estimated value exceeds SLE 300 million for procurement of goods and services (SLE 600 million
for works).

3.122. Bidders that are dissatisfied with the procurement decision have the right to challenge or
appeal at any stage of the process. Initially, complaints can be submitted through an application for
reconsideration to the head of the procuring entity. If the bidder remains unsatisfied, it may request
a review by the Independent Procurement Review Panel and, if needed, escalate the appeal to a
high court whose decision is final.

4 TRADE POLICIES BY SECTOR

4.1 Agriculture, forestry, and fisheries

4.1.1 Overview

4.1. Accounting for more than two thirds of GDP in 2023 and employing about three quarters of the
country's labour force, the agricultural sector is the backbone of the Sierra Leonean economy.

4.2. The sector's contribution to export earnings was less than 10% of total exports, with a heavy
reliance on cocoa. Imported food products account for a large share of domestic consumption.
Despite the enabling climate and arable agricultural land, rice and other major food products such
as wheat, flour, and poultry continue to be imported in large quantities.

4.3. Agriculture in Sierra Leone holds immense potential. Due to the country's significant rainfall
and vast expanses of fertile land, the agricultural sector has significant opportunities to enhance
productivity and profitability. Sierra Leone is endowed with some 5.4 million hectares of arable land,
with nearly 75% of it still available for cultivation.1 This favourable climate supports a diverse range
of crops and livestock farming. Food crops (predominantly rice and cassava) represent the largest
share of the country's agricultural production. However, during the review period, production data
for these crops showed significant variability, pointing to a fragile food security situation (Table 4.1).

4.5. Agricultural productivity and crop yields are hindered by various issues. Key factors include
declining soil fertility caused by land overuse, limited availability of agricultural inputs, and the
prevalence of pests and diseases. Additionally, the authorities highlight the lack of investment in
agricultural infrastructure, such as rural roads and irrigation systems, as significant barriers to
production. Furthermore, productivity is impacted by limited access to technical knowledge,
innovations, and advanced farming practices. Post-harvest losses are exacerbated by insufficient
storage facilities and drying floors, leaving farmers vulnerable to significant crop losses. According
to the authorities, post-harvest losses are estimated to average 40% and much higher for crops
including vegetables, fruits, cassava, and sweet potato. Limited access to financial services
constrains farmers from investing in agricultural inputs, with smallholders being particularly affected
due to the high cost of credit.3 As indicated at the time of the previous Review, Sierra Leone's land

4.1.2 Policy developments

4.7. The Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security oversees the overall development of the
agriculture sector.

4.8. The Medium-Term National Development Plan (MTNDP) 2019-2023 has served as the
overarching policy strategy framework for the Government to steer Sierra Leone towards improved
economic growth and development. Agriculture was an important policy objective under the MTNDP
2019-2023. Over that period, the authorities developed the National Agricultural Transformation
Policy 2019-2023 (NATP 2023), a strategy that aims to put agriculture at the centre of economic
development. It details plans for achieving the agricultural objectives of the MTNDP. The NATP 2023
focuses on developing agricultural value chains by increasing agricultural productivity and production
and establishing crops and livestock processing zones across the country. It also aims to enhance
market access and improve credit availability for marginalized groups, particularly women and rural
communities.4

4.9. The Government relied on foreign direct investment (FDI) to enhance productivity through
mechanized agricultural development and investments along the agriculture value chain. Under
NATP 2023, one of the key objectives was to increase public funding for agricultural projects to 10%
of the national budget within the first two years, while simultaneously encouraging private sector
investment. This initiative was supported by over USD 200 million in funding from international
organizations such as the World Bank, the FAO, and the European Union. At the time of the
NATP 2023's implementation, several commercial agricultural companies undertook operations in
Sierra Leone; engaged in the production or processing of products such as palm oil, timber, rice,
and seed sorghum; and provided agri-machinery contracting services. Other ventures included
pineapple cultivation, canning, and the manufacturing of juice concentrates.5

4.10. In 2023, as part of the agricultural component of Sierra Leone's MTNDP 2024-2030, the Feed
Salone Strategy (2023-2028) replaced the NATP as the overarching framework for agricultural
reform.6 The Strategy is structured around six strategic pillars aimed at enhancing mechanization
and irrigation systems; the seeds and inputs supply chain; aggregation, processing, and marketing
infrastructure; agricultural financing; the use of agricultural technology and climate-smart practices;
and empowering women and youth in the sector.

4.11. Key objectives include achieving self-sufficiency in staple foods such as rice, poultry, onions,
and cassava flour to reduce dependence on food imports; optimizing value chains for export-oriented
cash crops to boost foreign exchange earnings; developing agro-industrial zones dedicated to full-
scale production to create rural employment opportunities and support livestock farming, particularly
small ruminants; and implementing sustainable, climate-smart agricultural practices by improving
soil fertility, enhancing water management, diversifying crop production, and increasing resilience
to climate change.

seeds, with importation restricted to licensed seed sellers. Standards in Sierra Leone align with the
ECOWAS Harmonized Seed Regulations. However, despite these reforms, many farmers continue to
depend on the informal sector for seeds and frequently face challenges with seed quality and
mislabelling.8

4.14. In line with these reforms, the authorities adopted the National Fertilizer Policy in 2017,
transitioning from a government-led fertilizer strategy to one driven by the private sector. This policy
is aligned with the ECOWAS Regulation on fertilizer quality control, which aims to harmonize the
rules governing fertilizer quality across ECOWAS member States. The policy's objectives include
regulating and supervising fertilizer packaging, inspecting and analysing fertilizers distributed within
the country, overseeing the import and export of fertilizers, and issuing licences to fertilizer
importers, exporters, and manufacturers.

4.15. In 2024, import tariffs on agricultural products were 14.4% based on the WTO definition and
11.9% according to the ISIC classification. Agricultural inputs are generally low, varying from zero
to 5% for fertilizers and 5% to 10% for agricultural machineries.

4.16. Coffee, cocoa, palm kernel oil, palm kernel cake, ginger, and piassava are subject to export
levies (Section 3.2.2).

4.1.3 Forestry

4.17. The FAO considers Sierra Leone as moderately forested with 14.7% forest cover, and a further
61.1% of land classified as other wooded land.9 It also noted that over the next two decades, the
area of legally designated forest estates is expected to decline due to a heavy reliance on biomass-
based energy, logging, mining activities, and urban and agricultural expansion, which will
permanently alter land use. In situations involving mining encroachments, the forest land may be
replanted after mining operations conclude. Approximately 10% of the country's land area, including
government-controlled estates and salvage areas with closed forests, is projected to be deforested
at a rate of about 1.5% to 2.0% per year.10

4.18. No significant developments were registered in the forestry industry during the period under
review. In accordance with the country's MTNDP 2024-2030, the Government aims to ensure that
the national territory under sustainable carbon financing at least doubles from a baseline of
71,000 hectares, and an additional 10 million trees are planted. In 2019, a National Programme for
Tree Planting and Reforestation was established, to which the Government allocated initial funding

4.1.4 Fisheries

4.24. The fisheries sector is regulated by the Fisheries and Aquaculture Policy (2016), the Fisheries
Strategy (2016), the Fisheries and Aquaculture Act (2018), the Fisheries Fees Regulations (2018),
and the Fisheries Regulations (1995). Sierra Leone has a coastline of 506 km13, with a commercially
viable stock of fish. The fisheries sector is a vital component of the economy.

4.25. Fish catches during the review period were worth an estimated first-sale value of over
USD 200 million annually. As the main source of livelihood and animal protein, the sector provides
direct employment to some 200,000 persons and indirect employment to some 600,000 persons,
and it contributes over 10% to the country's GDP. The total annual production is estimated at
228,000 tonnes, with the marine artisanal catch contributing approximately 150,000 tonnes and
industrial catch production accounting for around 78,000 tonnes.14

4.26. The capital-intensive industrial fisheries subsector, primarily composed of foreign vessels,
operates in offshore waters. Common types of industrial vessels and fleets include purse seiners,
pelagic longliners, demersal and pelagic trawlers, and shrimp trawlers. Highly migratory tuna stocks
and deep-water rose shrimp, found mainly offshore, also attract high-seas fishing vessels. Fish

4.28. One of the main policy challenges is the effective control of fishing activities to prevent
overfishing, and the application of measures for a sustainable fishing industry and to align the total
fishing capacity with the allowable annual catch.17

4.29. The weakness of Sierra Leone's fisheries management system and its limited capability to
patrol its own EEZ has made the country highly vulnerable to illegal fishing by foreign fleets. This
poses a threat to the existence of the country's fisheries resources as it leads to both economic and
biological overexploitation. For the first time in the history of Sierra Leone, a closed season was
observed in April 2019. Throughout this one-month period, all industrial fishing vessels were
prohibited from fishing activities. This decision was made as part of a fisheries management strategy
and in response to concerns that the country's fisheries resources were being exploited in a
biologically unsustainable manner.

4.30. The economic and social impacts of IUU fishing at both national and community/household
levels include loss of revenues in terms of taxes, licence fees, export revenues, stock reduction,
unemployment, food insecurity, and expenditures for surveillance operations.

4.31. During the review period, Sierra Leone adopted the Fisheries and Aquaculture Act, 2018
(No. 10 of 2018), which provides general rules relating to commercial and artisanal fishing by
nationals of Sierra Leone wherever they are (beyond the fisheries waters, including fishing on the
high seas or in waters under the jurisdiction of another State) and commercial fishing by foreigners
in the fisheries waters of Sierra Leone and rules relating to aquaculture. The Act outlines the
objectives and goals for the management and development of fisheries. It mandates the Director of
Fisheries, in collaboration with relevant stakeholders, to implement annual Fisheries Management
Plans, subject to the Minister's prior approval, concerning any fishery resources or areas within
Sierra Leone's territorial waters. In 2019, a Fishery Management Plan for Sierra Leone (2020-2025)
and a National Plan of Action to Prevent, Deter and Eliminate Illegal, Unreported and Unregulated
Fishing were adopted.

4.32. To fish in Sierra Leone waters, a vessel must obtain a valid registration certificate issued by
the Director of Fisheries. Before importing, constructing, acquiring a new fishing vessel or gear, or
converting a vessel for fishing purposes, authorization from the Director of Fisheries is required. This
authorization ensures a thorough background check to confirm that the vessel and its prospective
owner have never participated in IUU fishing activities. A license or authorization issued by the
Director of Fisheries is mandatory before engaging in a specified set of activities. These activities
include, but are not limited to, using a fishing vessel (national or foreign) for fishing or related
purposes, employing a Sierra Leone fishing vessel for operations outside the fisheries waters,
operating a fish processing establishment or storage facility, exporting or importing live fish or other
fish products, and introducing any genetically modified fish into the fisheries waters.

4.33. The Act also addresses various aspects, including international and regional cooperation in
fisheries management to combat IUU fishing, as well as monitoring, control, and surveillance of
fisheries and related activities. It outlines enforcement measures regarding pollution in fisheries
waters, the development and regulation of aquaculture (covering issues like escape of aquaculture
stocks, use of drugs and chemicals, aquaculture waste, and transboundary aquaculture ecosystems),
and the Declaration of Protected or Endangered Aquatic Species. Additionally, it specifies offenses

4.37. During the review period, mining's contribution to GDP steadily rose from 2.1% in 2017 to
5.6% in 2023, due to the resumption of some mining activities notably iron ore. However, its
contribution to export earnings fluctuated over the same period, with a significant surge from 69.2%
in 2021 to 92.3% in 2023. The authorities aim to enhance governance and management within the
mining sector, focusing on value addition to promote employment, poverty reduction, community
benefits, environmental restoration, and revenue generation. Key goals include raising mining
revenue from 10% of domestic revenue in 2017 to 20% by 2030, increasing the mining sector's
contribution to GDP from 5% in 2017 to 20% by 2030, and completing environmental rehabilitation
at five mining sites across the country by 2030.18 As an action to assess the country's mineral
resources, the Government undertook a geophysical survey and collected data on the type, quantity,
and location of minerals in 2019.

4.38. The Ministry of Mines and Mineral Resources is responsible for managing, overseeing, and
regulating exploration, mining, and the trade of minerals. This includes formulating policies and
coordinating efforts between government agencies related to the development of the mineral sector.
The National Mineral Agency, established under the Ministry, is in charge of providing technical
support to the Ministry, including the execution, monitoring, and enforcement of licences.

4.39. Among reform steps, during the review period, the Parliament enacted the Mines and Mineral
Development Act, 2022, and its 2023 implementing Regulations19, which repealed the 2009 Mines
and Minerals Act. The reform is expected to enhance exploration, mining, and mineral development,
as well as the sale and export of minerals, to drive socio-economic benefits for the people of
Sierra Leone. It also promotes transparent and accountable management of the minerals sector,
aligning with international best practices. The Act mandates that companies secure free, prior, and
informed consent from local communities and landowners before initiating or continuing projects.
Additionally, during the review period, notable legislative reforms were implemented, including the
adoption of the Extractive Industries Revenue Act, 2018 and the Environment Protection Act, 2022.

4.40. Under the Mines and Minerals Act, all mineral resources are owned by the State, and their
exploitation is regulated through licences. These licences are divided into five categories:
reconnaissance, exploration, artisanal mining, small-scale mining, and large-scale mining, with
minimal restrictions for foreign entities. Artisanal mining is reserved exclusively for Sierra Leonean
citizens. However, reconnaissance, exploration, and large-scale mining licences are available to both

4.2.2 Energy

4.2.2.1 Overview

4.46. Biomass remains Sierra Leone's main source of energy, accounting for some 80% of the total
energy in 2017. Some 13% of energy demand was met by petroleum products and the remaining
by renewables. Biomass energy was predominantly used by households, while petroleum products
were mainly used in transportation.

4.47. The Ministry of Energy is responsible for oversight of the sector. However, other ministries
retain responsibility over some specific issues. For instance, the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and
Food Security is in charge of issues related to bioenergy and crop-related energy, and the Ministry
of Trade and Industry, through its Petroleum Regulatory Agency, deals with the marketing and sales
of petroleum products.

4.48. Under the MTNDP 2024-2030, the Government aims to provide adequate, affordable, and
sustainable power supply nationwide to stimulate economic development through the consolidation,
improvement, and expansion of existing energy supply infrastructure and ensure energy security for
sustainable development. The Plan's targets consist of increasing the percentage of the population
with access to electricity from 32% in 2021 to 60% by 2030, the percentage of households with
access to electricity from 25.4% in 2021 to 60% by 2030, and the number of district headquarters
                                       WT/TPR/S/470 • Sierra Leone

                                                    - 51 -


towns with reliable electricity supply from 9 in 2021 to 16 by 2030. An obsolete and inefficient
transmission and distribution network largely explains the low access to electricity in Sierra Leone.20

4.49. The strategic objective for the energy sector is to produce/generate and distribute adequate
electricity and power from renewable and clean sources for increased access for the population to
enhance economic growth and development. The Sierra Leone national energy policy document
(2009) remains the guiding framework for the Government's energy strategy. This policy is
complemented by the Renewable Energy Action Plan (2016) and the Bioenergy Action Plan (2022),
developed in alignment with the ECOWAS Bioenergy Policy. The authorities consider that improved
energy supply results in improved standards of living, which manifests in increased food production
and storage, increased industrial output, provision of efficient transportation and adequate shelter,
and improved healthcare and enhancements in other human services.

4.2.2.2 Petroleum

4.50. The upstream segment of the petroleum sector in Sierra Leone remains in the exploration
phase. The Government's vision for the development of the oil and gas industry involves investing
in geological data collection, enhancing pre-qualification criteria, and offering fiscal incentives to
attract foreign oil and gas companies.21 The Government has entered into a revenue-sharing
agreement with GeoPartners, a UK-based company, to conduct a seismic survey in areas that have
not yet been explored. This survey aims to improve understanding of subsurface structures and
reservoir characteristics.

4.51. The Petroleum Act is the primary legislation regulating petroleum exploration and production
activities in Sierra Leone. It grants the Republic of Sierra Leone ownership and control over
petroleum resources (crude oil, natural gas, or both) in their natural state. The Act outlines the
necessary licences for specific activities within the petroleum sector and establishes obligations
related to environmental protection, worker health and safety, and local participation.

petroleum licence, and a permit for the laying and operation of pipelines (Table 4.3).

4.2.2.3 Electricity

4.58. According to the authorities, the National Medium-Term Development Plan (NMTDP) 2019-
2023 led to substantial improvements in the electricity sector. Over the period 2019-23, considerable
investments in the generation, transmission, and distribution system yielded positive results, as
access to electricity more than doubled from 16% in 2018 to 36% in 2023 and households with
access nearly doubled from 13.5% in 2018 to 25.4% in 2021. The loss of electricity through leakages
in the distribution system increased from 38% in 2018 to 44% in 2019 and fell to 38% in 2021.
Official figures suggest that 9 district headquarters towns had a reliable electricity supply in 2021,
slightly below the target of 10 set in 2019. However, the percentage of the population with access
to environmental-friendly stoves increased from 18% in 2018 to 20.2% in 2019.23

4.59. The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission Act, 2011 and the National
Electricity Act, 2011 are the primary regulations governing the sector. The Electricity and Water
Regulatory Commission (EWRC) oversees licensing, monitors compliance, regulates tariffs, and
ensures fair competition for unregulated services. The industry is dominated by two state-owned
enterprises (SOEs): the Electricity Generation and Transmission Company (EGTC) and the Electricity
Distribution and Supply Authority (EDSA). The EGTC handles electricity generation and transmission,
while EDSA, as a monopoly, is responsible for electricity distribution. The Act allows independent
power producers (IPPs) to build generating stations, including hydroelectric projects, and to own
and operate transmission facilities. However, they are mandated to sell their generated electricity
to EDSA. As of end 2024, seven IPPs were in operation in Sierra Leone.

4.60. The IMF identifies the lack of reliable energy supply as a major obstacle to economic and
social development.24 EDSA has been struggling with persistent commercial and technical losses,
estimated at around 38%. Its financial situation has worsened due to rising electricity procurement
costs, compounded by the depreciation of the local currency, which increased the value of dollar-
denominated obligations to Karpowership. Despite these challenges, electricity tariffs have remained
unchanged since 2019, putting additional strain on the Government's budget, with subsidies
standing at USD 42 million in 2022.

4.61. The authorities are implementing broad energy sector reforms, supported by the World Bank,
to enhance EDSA's operational efficiency, boost generation capacity, and improve the electricity
connection infrastructure. In 2022, to increase revenue, the Government had plans to review
electricity tariffs while preserving the social tariff band and integrate two mining companies into the
grid. EDSA aims to reduce losses to 35% by replacing old meters to combat power theft and installing

4.3 Manufacturing

4.62. Sierra Leone's manufacturing sector is relatively small, and its contribution to GDP gradually
declined during the review period, from 2% in 2017 to 1.6% in 2023 (Table 1.1). The sector employs
roughly 2% of the labour force and consists mainly of small and medium-sized enterprises. Activities
are limited to light manufacturing destined for domestic consumption.

4.63. The manufacturing sector in Sierra Leone faces significant obstacles, including low
technological development, high operational costs due to inadequate infrastructure, unreliable and
expensive energy, and limited access to financing. To address these challenges, efforts are being
made to improve Sierra Leone's integration into agri-business value chains, particularly in palm oil
processing.

4.64. Although several incentive schemes have been introduced to stimulate the sector, their
effectiveness appears to fall short in overcoming the many challenges presented by the country's
business environment.

4.65. In 2024, the average tariff rate applied to manufacturing products stood at 11.9%. The
highest tariff rates apply to dairy products, beverages and tobacco, textiles, and clothing. Additional
protection is provided by the excise tax, since it applies only to some imported manufactured goods
(Section 3).

4.4 Services

4.4.1 Financial services

4.66. In 2023, Sierra Leone's financial services sector accounted for approximately 3% of GDP,
gradually down from around 5% in 2018. The BSL regulates and supervises activities of commercial
banks and other financial institutions to ensure sound economic and financial environment, while
the Sierra Leone Insurance Commission (SLICOM) regulates and supervises the insurance sector.

4.67. As of December 2023, the BSL supervised 14 commercial banks, 44 licensed microfinance
institutions (MFIs), 17 CBs, 59 financial services associations, 79 foreign exchange bureaus,
2 discount houses, 1 home mortgage finance company, 1 leasing company, 2 mobile financial
services, and 1 stock exchange.27

4.68. The World Bank's figures indicate that the financial sector is highly concentrated.28
Commercial banks account for 85% of financial sector assets, and the largest two state-owned banks
alone hold about 30% of total financial sector assets.

4.69. The World Bank has also identified crowding-out by government securities as a key factor
contributing to the extremely limited credit market for the private sector in Sierra Leone. In 2020,
private sector credit amounted to just 6% of GDP. The lack of private lending is primarily due to
banks' preference for lending to the Government, where they can earn high returns. Consequently,
nearly half of commercial banks' assets are tied up in government securities. This dynamic also
discourages banks from offering innovative financial products, such as those with longer terms,
reduced collateral demands, or a focus on climate adaptation. 29

4.70. Sierra Leone's total financial inclusion rate remains low. Only 29% of adults have an account
with a formal financial institution or mobile money provider, well below the Sub-Saharan African
average (55%). A gender gap is also noticeable, with just 25% of women owning an account,
compared to 33% of men. The main barriers include insufficient funds (89% of the adult population),
lack of documentation (32%), high costs (22%), and distance to providers (20%).30

4.71. Achieving financial inclusion is a key priority for the authorities. In 2016, Sierra Leone
launched its first National Strategy for Financial Inclusion (NSFI), implemented from 2017 to 2020,
aiming to make financial services accessible and affordable to all Sierra Leoneans and MSMEs and
fostering inclusive, resilient, private-sector-led growth. The Strategy led to the establishment of the
Financial Inclusion Secretariat within the BSL, tasked with overseeing its implementation.

4.72. Key achievements under the NSFI include the organization of a Fintech Challenge, the creation
of a Regulatory Sandbox, and the development of a Financial Literacy Framework. Other notable
initiatives include the establishment of the Sierra Leone Collateral Registry (SLCR). The SLCR, an
online platform, enables the registration of security interests in both movable and immovable
property, reducing risks for lenders by preventing the same property from being used as collateral
for multiple loans. The 2019 Borrowers and Lenders Act further expanded the scope of the Collateral
Registry, allowing for the registration of immovable assets and security interests from lenders not
regulated by the BSL. Additionally, under the Strategy, the BSL initiated efforts to implement a
national retail payment switch and establish a deposit insurance scheme, with the adoption during
the review period of the related National Payment Systems Act, 2022; National Switch Directives,
2023; and Deposit Protection Fund Act (DPF Act), 2023. Among the progress made in strengthening
Sierra Leone's financial services industry, the authorities have established the Financial Policy
Committee, approved new Corporate Governance Guidelines for the banking sector, and established
a Deposit Protection Fund Unit in the BSL to fully operationalize the DPF Act.

4.73. However, the evaluation of the NSFI revealed challenges in execution, including inefficient
working groups, a lack of accountability, and insufficient technical expertise. The absence of gender-
disaggregated data and measurement mechanisms hindered the accurate assessment of the
strategy's success. These shortcomings were primarily due to limited ownership, human capacity,
and financial resources.

4.74. In 2022, the BSL launched its NSFI for 2022-2026.31 The Strategy aims to target women,
youth, rural populations, and MSMEs. Its objectives include expanding access to client-centric
financial products and services for underserved groups, leveraging innovative technology to increase
access to affordable and safe digital financial services, and ensuring all Sierra Leoneans receive
consistent financial education, thus improving their financial literacy and confidence in using financial
services safely.

4.4.1.1 Banking

4.75. Sierra Leone's banking system is small relative to the size of the economy. In 2024, there
were 14 commercial banks in operation: 10 were wholly foreign-owned, 2 had foreign participation,
and 2 were state-owned.

4.76. The banking sector is highly concentrated, with the five largest banks making up 66% of total
banking sector assets. In 2020, total credit to the private sector represented 6% of the country's
GDP. Banks accounted for 5.9% of GDP in credit, with the remainder provided by microfinance
institutions and non-bank financial institutions.

4.77. The banking sector remains stable but faces vulnerabilities due to its high exposure to the
Government and a challenging macroeconomic environment. According to the BSL, credit risk in the
loan portfolio was heightened in 2021, largely due to the lingering impacts of COVID-19 restrictions
on various economic activities. The ratio of non-performing loans (NPLs) rose to 15.2% in 2021, up
from 12.7% at the end of 2020, exceeding the regulatory target of 10%. Notably, the NPL ratio for
the mining, quarrying, and business services sectors worsened significantly, climbing to 32% and

4.78. The legal framework was subject to various reforms. For example, the Anti-Money Laundering

The Redenomination of the Leone Regulations, 2021 was implemented to simplify transactions

4.80. Banks are subject to minimum paid-up capital, capital adequacy ratios, and liquidity

banks from SLE 85 million to SLE 270 million. Commercial banks are expected to meet this new
requirement progressively over a three-year period.34 The minimum capital adequacy ratio for risk-
weighted assets and the cash ratio equivalent for total deposits remain unchanged at 15% and 12%,
respectively. The authorities indicate that they are working to develop prudential standards in line
with the Basel Core Principles II/III.

4.81. As at the time of the previous Review, the Banking Act outlines the process for licensing
banking businesses, regulating banking activities, and protecting depositors. Any institution wishing
to operate a bank must apply for a licence from the BSL. Eligible applicants include registered
companies, cooperative societies, and statutory bodies. Foreign banks can only operate through a
subsidiary, subject to confirmation from their home supervisory authority that they meet
management and prudential standards. Licences are issued for at least five years and are non-
transferable. During the review period, the Banking Act, 2011 was replaced by the Banking Act,
2019. The new Act introduces holding companies as entities subject to licensing, regulation, and
supervision requirements, alongside banks. It also prioritizes the protection of depositors.35

4.82. The microfinance sector in Sierra Leone is diverse, encompassing four types of institutions
that serve similar client bases: credit-only MFIs, deposit-taking MFIs, member-based CBs, and
financial services associations (i.e. credit cooperatives pending licensing). The sector is regulated
through the Other Financial Services Act, 2001, with its implementing regulations.36

4.83. Many MFIs are small with limited assets. Out of 57 licensed credit-only MFIs, only 25 regularly
report to the BSL. Additionally, there are 23 deposit-taking institutions, including 5 deposit-taking
MFIs, 17 CBs, and Apex Bank (SL) Ltd, which oversees 59 FSAs under its purview awaiting BSL
licensing.37 The industry saw significant growth in 2021, with MFIs experiencing a 40% increase in
assets, and deposit-taking MFIs reporting a 43% rise in assets, equity, and deposits. In the first

4.4.1.2 Insurance

4.84. The legal framework for the insurance industry remained largely unchanged during the review
period. The Insurance Act, 2016 continues to regulate insurance operations in Sierra Leone. The
country's insurance market encompasses 11 insurance companies. The market also includes
11 insurance brokers and 10 insurance agents. The West African Insurance Companies Association
(WAICA) Re-Insurance Corporation was, for years, the only registered re-insurance company. In
2023, a second re-insurance company was brought into operation.

4.85. Under the Insurance Act, anyone operating an insurance business must obtain a licence. As
noted in the previous Review, a company can engage in multiple classes of business but must
maintain separate accounts for each. Commercial banks can be licensed as corporate insurance
agents for general insurers, considering their role in selling insurance products to borrowers as part
of their lending requirements. Registration is also required for insurance agents, brokers, adjusters,
and assessors, with licences issued annually. Additionally, insurance companies must pay an annual
levy of 1% of their gross premiums to SLICOM. Brokers are required to pay a levy at a rate of 0.5%
of their commission, despite the actual rate being 3%.

4.86. Insurers are mandated to keep their assets at least 10% higher than their liabilities based on
premium income. Specifically for life insurers, liabilities must not exceed the amount held in life
insurance funds. Companies are prohibited from declaring or distributing dividends until they have
met all obligations regarding paid-up capital, solvency, and reserves. Additionally, they are required
to invest a minimum of 25% of their premiums in government securities, while the threshold is set
at 50% for companies involved in life insurance.

4.87. There are no restrictions on companies established in Sierra Leone when it comes to insuring
risks located outside the country. However, foreign companies must register locally to insure risks
within Sierra Leone. While insurance companies set their own premiums, these are subject to
approval by SLICOM. Re-insurance policies are to be contracted with Sierra Leonean reinsurers.

4.88. The following insurance classes remain compulsory in Sierra Leone motor third-party liability
insurance, marine liability insurance, property insurance, social security insurance, and professional
indemnity insurance. Importers are also required to ensure their imports with a company registered
in Sierra Leone.

4.4.2 Telecommunications

4.89. The Government's priority in the telecommunications industry is to improve digital
penetration, access, and literacy and increase uptake through policies to expand coverage and
infrastructure across the country. According to the authorities, the National Broadband Strategy
2023-2028 aims at achieving 50% digital penetration in urban areas by 2027 and 95% by 2030,
improving rural connectivity to 80% by 2027, increasing digital literacy in schools to 85%, and
reaching 50% digital literacy among the national workforce by 2027. The authorities indicate that
the upgrading of mobile network operators' sites from 2G to 3G and 4G technology and the recent
5G trial licence granted to two operators (Africell and Orange) have further aided the increase in
digital penetration from under 10% before 2019 to about 30% in 2024. The penetration rates for
mobile, fixed-line, and Internet services grew steadily between 2017 and 2024, increasing from
87.7% to 98.5%, 0.2% to 0.9%, and 13.2% to 38%, respectively.

4.90. In 2021, a National Digital Development Strategy was adopted, presenting a plan to harness
digital technologies for socio-economic progress in Sierra Leone.39 It highlights the critical role of
infrastructure, skills development, governance, and cybersecurity in creating a resilient and inclusive
digital economy. The Strategy calls for the creation of a national digital infrastructure master plan,
encourages infrastructure sharing, and aims to improve universal access to digital services,
especially in underserved areas. It also focuses on enhancing the affordability of digital services and
devices while addressing the environmental impact of digital growth. Additionally, the Strategy
acknowledges the potential of digital financial services and e-commerce to lower transaction costs

4.92. The Internet industry in Sierra Leone consists of the monopoly Cable Landing Station

4.93. The National Communications Authority Act, 2022 mandates that telecommunications
operators ensure interconnection among their networks. Upon receiving a written request from one
operator, another operator is required to interconnect and engage in good faith negotiations to
establish the terms of the interconnection agreement. This process aims to facilitate the provision
of electronic communications services to consumers.

4.94. Operators have the freedom to negotiate interconnection arrangements independently.
However, if any challenges arise, they can notify the regulator, which will investigate the matter.
The regulator's role is to promote fair, transparent, and seamless interconnection practices among
operators.

4.95. The Ministry of Information and Communications (MIC) oversees national policies related to
information and communications. In September 2022, Parliament enacted the National
Communications Authority Act, which replaced the Telecommunications Act, 2006 as the primary
legislation governing the sector. This law established the National Communications Authority
(NatCA) as the regulatory body for telecommunications in Sierra Leone, succeeding the National
Telecommunications Commission (NATCOM). NatCA's responsibilities include licensing and
regulating ICT operators, promoting universal access to basic telecommunications services, fostering
fair competition to benefit ICT network and service investors, protecting users of ICT networks and
services, and resolving disputes between operators and consumers. The licensing regime has shifted
from technology-specific licences to technology-neutral unified licences.

4.96. The authorities indicate that five regulations were drafted by the Authority with supervision
from the MIC and ratified by Parliament in December 2020:

      •   Telecommunications Licensing Regulations, 2020, to establish a framework for licensing
          telecommunications operators, ensuring fair competition and efficient service delivery;

      •   Electronic Communications Equipment Type Approval Regulations, to ensure electronic
          communications equipment used in Sierra Leone meets the required standards and is safe
          for use;

      •   Radio Frequency Spectrum Regulations, to manage and allocate radio frequency spectrum
          efficiently, ensuring optimal use and minimizing interference;

      •   Telecommunications Quality of Service Regulations, to set and enforce standards for the
          quality of telecommunications services provided to consumers; and

      •   Subscriber Identification and Registration Management Regulations, to provide a clear
          framework for identifying and verifying subscribers and ensuring data accuracy and
          security for legal and national security purposes.

4.4.3 Transport

4.4.3.1 Overview

4.100. The Ministry of Transport and Aviation is responsible for overarching policy formulation for
the transport sector.

4.101. The authorities' vision is to create a resilient and sustainable transport sector that drives
socio-economic growth, enhances international competitiveness, and fosters human capital
development in Sierra Leone. Their goal is to establish strategic policies and provide clear policy
guidelines for delivering safe, secure, reliable, affordable, sustainable, and resilient maritime, land,
and aviation transportation systems across the country.

4.102. The policy and strategic steps for Sierra Leone's three transport modes are anchored by four
main pillars: ensuring effective sector governance to improve transportation services; modernizing
public transport and enhancing road safety; providing safe, secure, and efficient maritime services
to unlock the potential of the blue economy and facilitate trade; and strengthening aviation safety
while promoting global confidence in the industry. The expected outcome would be to reduce logistic
costs for the business community and ensure its competitiveness. The Government relies on PPPs
to fill the country's infrastructural gap, particularly for the construction of roads and airports.

4.4.3.2 Land transport

4.103. The Sierra Leone Road Safety Authority is responsible for making and enforcing road
transport regulations, including collecting road-user charges. The Authority's additional objectives
include coordinating relevant stakeholders and promoting efficiency within the road transport sector.

4.104. A dedicated Road Maintenance Fund was established to cover the costs associated with
maintaining the core road network. The Fund primarily derives its revenue from a road-user fee of
USD 0.16 per litre of fuel, as well as vehicle licensing, registration fees, and other charges collected
under the Road Traffic Act, 2007. The administration of the Fund is overseen by the Sierra Leone
Road Maintenance Fund Administration.

4.105. The authorities indicate that the industry is fully open to foreign participation.

4.106. The rail transportation system is operated solely by one mining company, with no public rail
services available. By contrast, Sierra Leone no longer has any general-purpose railroads for public
or commercial use.

4.4.3.3 Air transport

4.107. Aviation plays a crucial economic and social role in Sierra Leone's development. Most
visitors, including tourists, primarily rely on air transport. Air connectivity is vital for the country's
integration into GVCs through delivery of certain engineering services, while efficient air services

4.108. The Sierra Leone Civil Aviation Authority (SLCAA) has the mandate of overseeing air

4.109. The SLCAA is responsible for granting licences for international and domestic scheduled and

in commercial air transport services (including foreign air operators) require a licence from the
SLCAA. For air operators to be granted air traffic rights by the SLCAA, they must comply with the
applicable technical requirements. Airlines currently flying to Sierra Leone include Brussels Airlines,
Turkish Airlines, Asky, Kenyan Airways, and Royal Air Maroc.

4.110. The Sierra Leone Airport Authority, an SOE, manages Lungi International Airport and all the
other national airfields. Most of the services at Lungi International Airport are provided by private
entities, with the exception of the tour operator. However, service fees at the airport are significantly
higher than the regional average. This has led to high air ticket prices for flights operating through
the airport. As a result, some passengers opt to fly to neighbouring countries and use alternative
transportation methods to reach Sierra Leone, finding it more cost-effective. Currently there is no
national carrier.

4.111. Sierra Leone is a signatory to the Yamoussoukro Declaration concerning the liberalization of
intra-African air transport markets. Sierra Leone is also a party to the Banjul Accord Group
Agreement, together with Cabo Verde, Ghana, and the Gambia. Sierra Leone has concluded 22 air
services agreements involving 42 countries.41

4.4.3.4 Maritime transport

4.112. Maritime transport is the primary mode of transportation for Sierra Leone's international
trade in goods. In 2023, the World Bank ranked Sierra Leone's Freetown Port 226th out of 405 global
ports, maintaining the same position as in 2022 when 348 ports were evaluated. In Sub-Saharan
Africa, Freetown Port, the main national harbour, was ranked 5th out of 40 container ports according
to the World Bank's Container Port Performance Index 2023.42

4.113. In their strategic vision to enhance the efficiency of maritime transport industry and port
activities, the authorities aim at, inter alia, increasing access to inland waterway transportation
services through PPPs, streamlining the clearing process at the port, and reducing the turnaround
time for clearing and forwarding from 72 hours to 24 hours.

4.114. During the review period, the Sierra Leone Port and Harbour Authority Act (Act No. 29 of
2023) established the Sierra Leone Ports and Harbours Authority, which replaced the Sierra Leone
Ports Authority. It manages and coordinates activities at the country's seaports and main river
ports.43

4.4.4 Tourism

4.119. Tourism development is a key priority for Sierra Leone in its efforts to diversify the economy.
The country's first-ever National Strategic Tourism Plan was adopted in May 2024.44

4.120. The authorities are committed to fostering sustainable tourism as a catalyst for economic
growth and socio-cultural integration while positioning the country as a welcoming tourist
destination. Their vision is to create a dynamic and appealing brand of sustainable tourism that
highlights the natural environment; cultural heritage; artisanal craftsmanship; and rural lifestyles.
This initiative aims to benefit visitors, residents, and communities alike, ensuring the long-term
prosperity of Sierra Leone. By 2030, the MTNDP 2024-2030 aims to boost international arrivals by
at least 50% annually and create a 30% increase in job opportunities within the tourism sector.

4.121. According to the authorities, the Government's priorities and challenges in the tourism sector
include enhancing the policy and legal framework governing the industry; fostering competition to
lower costs and improve value for money by raising standards and service quality; promoting eco-
tourism to safeguard the nation's rich biodiversity and encourage green investments; supporting
local entrepreneurs and establishing connections with international partners to facilitate tourism-
related activities that align with the rural economy, harvest seasons, wildlife tourism, agricultural
practices, and creative arts and culture; and bolstering public-private community partnerships while
encouraging greater involvement from the private sector.

4.122. A sector-specific incentive programme is available to encourage investments in tourism
(Section 3.3.1).

4.123. According to the authorities, in 2022, direct employment in the tourism sector stood at
